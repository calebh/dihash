\begin{abstract}
We study structural hashing of vertex-labeled directed graphs, with a focus on precisely characterizing when two vertices receive the same hash. Global hashing identifies graphs up to isomorphism and vertices up to automorphism orbits; local hashing identifies isomorphic infinite unfoldings or, in a variant, bisimilar vertices. To handle cycles, we introduce a general Merkle construction that hashes each strongly connected component (SCC) and propagates its hashes through the acyclic condensation graph. We prove a correctness theorem for arbitrary component equivalences and locate the resulting global and local Merkle equivalences strictly between rooted reachable-subgraph isomorphism and unfolding isomorphism. Combining the local Merkle construction with canonical color refinement on the unfolding quotient gives cross-graph comparable hashes for unfolding isomorphism and bisimilarity in $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ combinatorial time for $n$ vertices and $m$ explicitly represented edge occurrences, plus label-processing and serialization costs. For graphs with ordered outgoing edges, we give linear-time rooted hashing and canonize strongly connected graphs in $O((n+m)\log(n+m)+c(n+m))$ combinatorial time, where $c$ is the size of a selected smallest unfolding-equivalence class. We also prove that canonization of general ordered digraphs is graph-isomorphism hard and that hashing vertices by their walk languages is PSPACE-hard. We provide an implementation, correctness checks, benchmarks, and a content-addressed website application sketch.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Content-addressed storage identifies an object by a hash of its contents. For a tree or an acyclic dependency graph, Merkle hashing makes this construction compositional: each object's hash incorporates the hashes of the objects it references \cite{merkle1987digital,git,ipfs}. For directed graphs with cycles, however, this recursion is not well founded. More fundamentally, there is no single notion of when two graph vertices should be considered the same: an application may care about their positions in an entire graph, their reachable structure, or only the observations made by repeatedly following outgoing edges.

We study hashing of vertex-labeled directed graphs with exact structural guarantees. The central question is: \emph{which equivalence relation is characterized by equality of two node hashes?} Global graph isomorphism distinguishes structures that local observations cannot; the isomorphism classes of infinite unfoldings distinguish multiplicities that bisimulation ignores. Between these extremes, our Merkle constructions hash strongly connected components (SCCs) as units and propagate their hashes along the acyclic condensation graph. We define the induced equivalences independently of the hash function and prove that the algorithms realize them, modulo cryptographic collisions (Section~\ref{sec:hash-model}).

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Recursive Merkle-tree Hashing of Ordered Binary Tree}
\[
\text{mh}(n) =
\begin{cases}
    \mathcal{H}(n.\text{label}) & n \text{ is a leaf} \\
    \mathcal{H}(n.\text{label} \circ \text{mh}(n.\text{left}) \circ \text{mh}(n.\text{right})) & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]
\label{alg:merklehashing}
\end{algorithm}

Hashing functions can be extended to trees via a simple extension of Merkle hashing \cite{merkle1987digital}. In Merkle tree hashing, the hash of a node is computed by hashing the concatenation of the node's label with the recursive hash of the node's children. Algorithm \ref{alg:merklehashing} gives a simple recursive implementation of Merkle tree hashing for ordered binary trees. This particular variation of Merkle hashing has seen use in many applications, including cryptocurrency \cite{bitcoin}, version control systems \cite{git}, and distributed hypertext document storage systems \cite{ipfs}. For \emph{unordered} trees, replacing the sequence of child hashes by a sorted multiset of child hashes yields a hash that is invariant under tree isomorphism, in the spirit of the classical Aho--Hopcroft--Ullman tree isomorphism algorithm \cite{aho1974design}.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:merklehashing} breaks down on a directed cycle because the recursive calls do not terminate. Collapsing SCCs solves the termination problem but leaves the choice of an equivalence inside each SCC. Our constructions address both questions. The following schemes make different, explicitly stated choices about what a hash should preserve:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Global Hashing} (Section \ref{sec:global-graph-hashing}) --- for use cases where the \textit{global} structure of the digraph is of importance. Two graphs hash equally if and only if they are isomorphic, and two nodes hash equally if and only if some isomorphism of their graphs maps one onto the other; in particular, nodes in the same automorphism orbit hash equally.
  \item \textbf{Merkle Graph Hashing} (Section \ref{sec:merkle-graph-hashing}) --- for use cases where graphs are hashed recursively, or are built up over time. We hash strongly connected components individually with a component hash, then recurse over the acyclic condensation graph, and characterize precisely which nodes receive equal hashes for any component hash. With the global node hash of each component this gives an equivalence that lies strictly between rooted isomorphism and unfolding isomorphism.
  \item \textbf{Local Hashing} (Section \ref{sec:local-graph-hashing}) --- for use cases where observers can only observe \textit{local} information by traversing from a node to a neighbor on an outgoing edge. Two nodes hash equally if and only if the (potentially infinite) trees obtained by unfolding the graph from them are isomorphic. We obtain this hash from the Merkle construction, with a canonical local component hash, applied to the quotient of the graph by unfolding isomorphism. We also give a bisimilarity variant; both have $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ combinatorial running time on explicit-edge inputs, plus label and serialization costs (Section~\ref{sec:cost-model}). Additionally, we show that hashing the language of vertex walks is PSPACE-hard. Local hashing has many commonalities with bisimulation and co-inductive representations of finite directed graphs \cite{bisimulation_book,coinductive_graphs}.
  \item \textbf{Ordered Outgoing Edges} (Section \ref{sec:outgoing-ordered-edge-graphs}) --- in many practical applications the outgoing edges from a node are ordered. We use this additional structure to obtain linear-time node hashes. For strongly connected graphs, we give an algorithm for computing both a canonical order and orbit partition in near-linear time for most graphs, and quadratic time in the worst case. This algorithm can then be plugged into our Merkle hashing approach to obtain an efficient global Merkle hash. For general ordered digraphs, canonization is as hard as for graphs.
\end{itemize}

One common theme throughout this paper is reliance on conversion of graphs to \textit{canonical form}. If two graphs (or nodes within them) can be converted to a canonical form, we can serialize the canonical form and pass it through an ordinary string hashing function. We distinguish carefully between the hash of an entire graph and the hash of a specific node within a graph. Our general strategy for computing the hash of a specific node is to combine the overall graph hash with the node's position in canonical order, taking care that this position accounts for any symmetry present in the graph (two nodes may be identical modulo automorphism).

\paragraph{Contributions.} Our main results are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item A general SCC-based Merkle theorem characterizing hash equality for any component hash that realizes an injective-relabeling-invariant equivalence (Theorem~\ref{thm:merkle}), together with a strict hierarchy of structural equivalences (Proposition~\ref{prop:merkle-position}).
  \item Cross-graph comparable local hashes for unfolding isomorphism and bisimilarity (Theorems~\ref{thm:localhashcorrect} and~\ref{thm:bisimhash}), using canonical color refinement \cite{berkholz2017tight} and the Merkle hash of the unfolding quotient. Both run in $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ combinatorial time, in addition to label and serialization costs (Section~\ref{sec:cost-model}).
  \item A linear-time rooted hash for graphs with ordered outgoing edges, and SCC canonization whose work depends on the size of a smallest unfolding-equivalence class (Theorems~\ref{thm:orderedhash} and~\ref{thm:scc-ordered}); we also show the difficulty of canonizing general ordered digraphs (Proposition~\ref{prop:ordered-gi}).
  \item Characterizations of the local Merkle equivalence by SCC-annotated unfoldings (Proposition~\ref{prop:annotated}) and of the hardness of hashing walk languages (Proposition~\ref{prop:pspace}).
\end{enumerate}

For roots compared by their reachable subgraphs, the relations form the strict implication chain
\[
\text{rooted reachable-subgraph isomorphism}
\;\Longrightarrow\; \eqg
\;\Longrightarrow\; \eql
\;\Longrightarrow\; \text{unfolding isomorphism}
\;\Longrightarrow\; \text{bisimilarity}.
\]
Proposition~\ref{prop:merkle-position} proves the first three implications are strict; the final one is strict because bisimulation does not count duplicated bisimilar successors. In contrast, the global node hash requires isomorphism of the \emph{entire} input graphs. For clarity, the nauty-based global node and global Merkle schemes are specified for simple digraphs; the general Merkle theorem and the local schemes also handle explicitly represented parallel edges. The ordered-outgoing-edge variants are treated separately in Section~\ref{sec:outgoing-ordered-edge-graphs}.

\subsection{Labeled digraphs and symmetry.}
A \emph{labeled digraph} is a triple $G=(V,E,L)$ where $V$ is a finite vertex set, $E \subseteq V \times V$ is the edge set (self-loops are allowed), and $L$ maps vertices to encodable labels, including strings and structured values. From Section~\ref{sec:merkle-graph-hashing} on, we also use \emph{labeled multidigraphs} $G=(V,\mu,L)$, where $\mu : V\times V \to \mathbb{N}$ gives edge multiplicities; a digraph is the special case $\mu(u,v)\in\{0,1\}$. Labels are compared by equality and, where necessary, by the lexicographic order of their injective encodings. We write $\Succ(u)$ for the multiset of successors of $u$ (with $v$ occurring $\mu(u,v)$ times), and $\Reach(u)$ for the set of vertices reachable from $u$ (including $u$). A set $S \subseteq V$ is \emph{forward-closed} if every successor of a vertex of $S$ lies in $S$; each $\Reach(u)$ is forward-closed, and so is $V$.

An \emph{isomorphism} $\phi : G_1 \to G_2$ is a bijection $V_1 \to V_2$ with $L_1(u) = L_2(\phi(u))$ and $\mu_1(u,v) = \mu_2(\phi(u),\phi(v))$ for all $u,v$; we write $G_1 \iso G_2$. A \emph{rooted isomorphism} $(G_1,v_1) \iso (G_2,v_2)$ is an isomorphism with $\phi(v_1)=v_2$. An \emph{automorphism} is an isomorphism $G\to G$; the automorphisms form the group
\[
\Aut(G) = \{ \sigma : V \to V \text{ bijective} \mid L(\sigma(u)) = L(u),\ \mu(\sigma(u),\sigma(v))=\mu(u,v)\ \text{for all }u,v\in V\}.
\]
For a simple digraph this condition reduces to $(u,v)\in E\iff(\sigma(u),\sigma(v))\in E$; for a multidigraph it also preserves parallel-edge multiplicities. Informally, the automorphism group gives all of the ways that $G$ is symmetrical. The \emph{orbit} of a vertex is the set of vertices it can be mapped to by automorphisms,
\[
\Orb(G, v) = \{\sigma(v) \mid \sigma \in \Aut(G)\},
\]
and the orbits partition $V$. Figure~\ref{fig:orbitexample} gives an example. Note that $v_1$ and $v_2$ lie in the same orbit of $G$ if and only if $(G,v_1)\iso(G,v_2)$.

\subsection{Canonical forms.}
Fix a canonical labeling procedure for simple digraphs (for example nauty/Traces \cite{nauty,mckay2014practical}, bliss \cite{bliss}, or saucy \cite{saucy}, with vertex colors representing labels). The global algorithms in Section~\ref{sec:global-graph-hashing} are deliberately restricted to simple digraphs, consistent with our nauty-based implementation. Multidigraph canonization could instead be reduced to canonization of vertex-colored simple digraphs by introducing an edge vertex for each occurrence of a parallel edge, with directed incidence edges and distinct vertex types to encode its source and target; this extension is not implemented here. Given $G$ with $|V|=n$ these algorithms return a bijection $\rho : V \to \{0,\dots,n-1\}$ such that the relabeled graph $\canon(G) = \rho(G)$ satisfies
\[
\canon(G_1) = \canon(G_2) \iff G_1 \iso G_2 .
\]
By construction $\rho$ is an isomorphism $G\to\canon(G)$. It is determined only up to automorphisms: if $\rho$ is a canonical labeling then so is $\rho\circ\sigma$ for every $\sigma \in \Aut(G)$, and no canonical labeling procedure can prefer one of these over another. This is precisely why node hashes must be defined through orbits rather than through canonical indices alone.

\subsection{Strongly connected components.}
A \textit{strongly connected component} (SCC) of a digraph is a maximal set of vertices such that there is a path between any two of them. The \textit{condensation graph} is formed by contracting each SCC into a single node; it is acyclic, and all SCCs together with the condensation can be computed in linear time \cite{tarjan1972depth}. The \emph{height} of an SCC is the number of edges on a longest path starting from it in the condensation. Figure \ref{fig:condensation} shows an example.

\subsection{Input representation and cost model.}
\label{sec:cost-model}
All combinatorial running-time bounds use a word-RAM model with word size $\Omega(\log(n+m))$. Here $n=|V|$, and $m=\sum_{u,v}\mu(u,v)$ counts \emph{edge occurrences}, including parallel edges, rather than just distinct ordered pairs. A multidigraph is represented by explicit adjacency lists containing each occurrence; the ordered model represents these occurrences by its out-sequences. Vertex identifiers, port indices, multiplicities at most $m$, canonical indices, and refinement counters fit in a word, and arithmetic and comparisons on these integers take constant time. A multidigraph whose multiplicities are supplied in binary as a compressed list of triples $(u,v,\mu(u,v))$ is \emph{not} covered by a bound measured in its compressed input length. In that representation, $m$ may be exponentially larger than the input length.

We distinguish graph operations from processing arbitrary encodable vertex labels and cryptographic data. Let $T_{\mathrm{lab}}$ denote the total cost of making labels available in a canonical, equality-testable and ordered representation, including reading or encoding original labels, grouping and sorting distinct labels, and comparisons or interning of derived structured labels. Let $T_{\mathrm{ser}}$ denote the total cost of materializing, comparing when necessary, and hashing the serialized canonical forms and node records. These are \emph{total costs for the algorithm being analyzed}, including repeated serializations when several roots are considered. The abstract word-RAM bounds charge comparisons of already-interned label identifiers and fixed-size digest values as constant time, but do not silently treat arbitrary-length strings or encodings as constant-size. Integer encodings also cost space and time proportional to their encoded lengths in a bit-complexity model. With bounded-size input labels, word-sized identifiers, constant-size digests, and word-cost encodings, $T_{\mathrm{lab}}+T_{\mathrm{ser}}$ is within the combinatorial bounds given below. For unbounded labels, the total time is the stated combinatorial bound \emph{plus} these two costs.

For simple digraphs $m\le n^2$; the same logarithmic simplification holds whenever $m\le n^{O(1)}$. Thus $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ becomes $O((n+m)\log n)$ under this assumption. We retain $\log(n+m)$ for general explicitly represented multidigraphs.

\subsection{The hashing model.}
\label{sec:hash-model}
All of our schemes serialize a finite structure and apply a string hash $\mathcal{H}$. We assume a serialization $\enc(\cdot)$ of nested tuples, lists, integers, strings and other encodable labels that is \emph{injective} (for example, prefixing every item with a type tag and its length, so that labels containing delimiter characters cannot cause ambiguity), and we encode a multiset as the sorted list of its elements. Since $\mathcal{H}$ maps into a finite set it cannot be injective, so we state our theorems in the following sense.

\begin{defn}[Correctness up to collisions]
\label{def:correct}
A hash $h$ \emph{realizes} an equivalence relation $\sim$ if (i) $x \sim y$ implies $h(x)=h(y)$, and (ii) whenever $h(x) = h(y)$ but $x \not\sim y$, the computations of $h(x)$ and $h(y)$ contain two distinct strings $s \ne s'$ with $\mathcal{H}(s) = \mathcal{H}(s')$.
\end{defn}

Condition (ii) says that a failure of the ``only if'' direction exhibits a collision of $\mathcal{H}$. We use the term \emph{ideal hash model} for the mathematical idealization in which different serialized strings receive different formal hash values. This does not claim that any fixed-length cryptographic hash is injective or provide a numerical collision bound. All subsequent statements relating hash equality to structural equivalence are understood in the ideal hash model; for a concrete hash function, they realize their equivalences in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:correct}.

\section{Global Graph Hashing}
\label{sec:global-graph-hashing}

\begin{algorithm}[b]
    \caption{Global Digraph Hash}\label{digraphhash}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Function{$\HG$}{$G=(V,E,L)$}
        \State $\rho \gets$ \textproc{canonize}($G$) \Comment{canonical labeling $\rho : V \to \{0,\dots,|V|-1\}$}
        \State adj\_list $\gets$ \textproc{sort}($[(\rho(u), \rho(v)) \mid (u,v) \in E]$)
        \State labels $\gets$ $[L(\rho^{-1}(i))$ \textbf{for} $i = 0, \dots, |V|-1]$
        \State \Return $\mathcal{H}$($\enc$((labels, adj\_list)))
    \EndFunction
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

In global graph hashing, we would like our computed hash values to be unique modulo graph isomorphism: $\HG(G_1)=\HG(G_2)$ if and only if $G_1 \iso G_2$. We canonize the input digraph and construct an adjacency list sorted in canonical order. The node labels (in canonical order) and the canonical adjacency list are then serialized and hashed using a string hashing function $\mathcal{H}$. Algorithm \ref{digraphhash} gives an implementation of this approach. Note that the pair (labels, adj\_list) is exactly an encoding of $\canon(G)$: the number of vertices is the length of the label list, so isolated vertices are accounted for.

\footnotetext{\url{https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Graph_Condensation.svg}}

\begin{theorem}[Correctness of the global graph hash]
\label{thm:digraphhashcorrect}
$\HG$ realizes graph isomorphism: in the ideal hash model, $\HG(G_1)=\HG(G_2)$ if and only if $G_1 \iso G_2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
($\Rightarrow$) If $\HG(G_1)=\HG(G_2)$, then by injectivity of $\mathcal{H}$ and $\enc$ the canonical label lists and adjacency lists coincide, i.e.\ $\canon(G_1)=\canon(G_2)$. Hence $G_1 \iso G_2$.

($\Leftarrow$) If $G_1 \iso G_2$ then $\canon(G_1)=\canon(G_2)$, so the label lists and sorted adjacency lists coincide, and so do their encodings and hashes.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Complexity.} Since $\HG$ decides isomorphism of labeled digraphs (in the idealized model), computing it is at least as hard as graph isomorphism (GI). No polynomial-time algorithm for GI is known; the best known worst-case bound is quasipolynomial, both for isomorphism testing \cite{babai2016quasipolynomial} and for canonization \cite{babai2019canonical}. Practical canonization tools based on individualization and refinement \cite{nauty,mckay2014practical,bliss,saucy} are extremely fast on most inputs, but have exponential worst-case running time on specially constructed families. The same remarks apply to computing orbits, which Algorithm~\ref{alg:digraph-node-hash} below requires; tools such as nauty return the orbit partition as a by-product of canonization.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
    \caption{Global Digraph Node Hash}\label{alg:digraph-node-hash}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Function{$\HN$}{$G$, $v$}
        \State $\rho \gets$ \textproc{canonize}($G$) \Comment{the same $\rho$ is used in the next line}
        \State g\_hash $\gets \HG(G)$ \Comment{computed from $\rho$ as in Algorithm~\ref{digraphhash}}
        \State orbit\_idx $\gets$ $\min\,\Orb(\canon(G), \rho(v))$
        \State \Return $\mathcal{H}$($\enc$((orbit\_idx, g\_hash)))
    \EndFunction
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The algorithm for hashing nodes builds on the algorithm for hashing graphs. First, the graph is canonized and the hash of the canonical graph is computed using Algorithm \ref{digraphhash}. Then we compute the \emph{orbit index} of the input vertex: the smallest canonical index in the orbit of $\rho(v)$ in the canonical graph $\canon(G)$. (Equivalently, it is $\min\{\rho(u) \mid u \in \Orb(G,v)\}$, since $\rho$ maps orbits of $G$ onto orbits of $\canon(G)$.) The orbit index and the graph hash are then combined and passed through a string hashing function. Algorithm \ref{alg:digraph-node-hash} gives an implementation of this approach. The orbit index does not depend on which canonical labeling $\rho\circ\sigma$, $\sigma\in\Aut(G)$, the canonizer happens to return, since $\rho(\sigma(v))$ lies in the same orbit of $\canon(G)$ as $\rho(v)$.

\begin{theorem}[Correctness of the global node hash]
\label{thm:digraphnodehashcorrect}
$\HN$ realizes rooted isomorphism: in the ideal hash model, $\HN(G_1, v_1)=\HN(G_2, v_2)$ if and only if there is an isomorphism $\phi : G_1 \to G_2$ with $\phi(v_1) = v_2$. In particular, two nodes of the same graph hash equally if and only if they lie in the same orbit.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\rho_1,\rho_2$ be the canonical labelings of $G_1,G_2$.

($\Leftarrow$) Since $G_1\iso G_2$, $C:=\canon(G_1)=\canon(G_2)$ and $\HG(G_1)=\HG(G_2)$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:digraphhashcorrect}. The map $\alpha = \rho_2\circ\phi\circ\rho_1^{-1}$ is a composition of isomorphisms $C \to G_1 \to G_2 \to C$, hence $\alpha\in\Aut(C)$, and $\alpha(\rho_1(v_1)) = \rho_2(v_2)$. Thus $\rho_1(v_1)$ and $\rho_2(v_2)$ lie in the same orbit of $C$, so the orbit indices coincide, and so do the node hashes.

($\Rightarrow$) Equal node hashes imply, by injectivity, equal graph hashes and equal orbit indices. By Theorem~\ref{thm:digraphhashcorrect} (and its proof) $\canon(G_1)=\canon(G_2)=:C$. Two orbits of $C$ with the same minimum element are the same orbit, so there is $\alpha \in \Aut(C)$ with $\alpha(\rho_1(v_1)) = \rho_2(v_2)$. Then $\phi = \rho_2^{-1}\circ\alpha\circ\rho_1$ is an isomorphism $G_1 \to G_2$ with $\phi(v_1)=v_2$.
\end{proof}

\section{Merkle Graph Hashing}
\label{sec:merkle-graph-hashing}

For applications where graphs are built up over time, a recursive approach similar to Merkle-tree hashing is highly beneficial. We now construct such an algorithm, which recurses over the condensation graph. The condensation is acyclic, so the recursion is well founded. The construction is parameterized by a \emph{component hash}, which hashes the nodes of a single strongly connected graph. With the global node hash of Section~\ref{sec:global-graph-hashing} it gives the global Merkle hash of this section. With the local component hash of Section~\ref{sec:local-graph-hashing} it gives the local Merkle hash, and, applied to the unfolding quotient, the local hash itself.

To compute the hash of each node, we process the strongly connected components in reverse topological order, so that every edge leaving a component points to nodes that have already been hashed. Each node $x$ of the component is relabeled by combining its label with the multiset $X(x)$ of the hashes of its external targets, counted with multiplicity. The new label $L'(x)=(L(x),X(x))$ is kept as a pair rather than hashed. The canonical form of a component therefore contains every label and every external hash verbatim, so the component, including its outgoing links, can be reconstructed from the value that is hashed (Section~\ref{sec:application} uses this). The labels $L'$ are structured values rather than strings. This is harmless, because the component hashes of Sections~\ref{sec:global-graph-hashing}, \ref{sec:local-graph-hashing} and~\ref{sec:outgoing-ordered-edge-graphs} use labels only through equality, sorting and the injective serialization $\enc$ function. Finally the relabeled component, restricted to its internal edges, is hashed with the component hash. Algorithm~\ref{alg:merklegraphhash} gives the pseudo-code; each component is processed once. Section~\ref{sec:local-graph-hashing} also uses a \emph{set variant}, in which $X(x)$ is the set rather than the multiset of external hashes, and Section~\ref{sec:outgoing-ordered-edge-graphs} adapts the algorithm to ordered graphs.

\begin{algorithm}
    \caption{Merkle Graph Node Hashing}\label{alg:merklegraphhash}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Function{$\HM$}{$G=(V,\mu,L)$, \textproc{comp\_hash}}
        \State $h \gets \textproc{empty\_dict}()$
        \For{\textbf{each} SCC $S$ in reverse topological order} \Comment{Tarjan's algorithm; sinks first}
          \For{$x \in S$}
            \State $X(x) \gets \mset{h[y] \mid y\notin S}$, where $h[y]$ is repeated $\mu(x,y)$ times \label{line:merkle-X}
            \State $L'(x) \gets (L(x), X(x))$ \Comment{not hashed}
          \EndFor
          \State $G' \gets (S,\ \mu|_{S\times S},\ L')$ \label{line:merkle-G}
          \State $h \gets h \cup \textproc{comp\_hash}(G')$ \Comment{hashes of all $x\in S$}
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $h$
    \EndFunction
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Which nodes receive equal hashes?}
Across components, a Merkle hash behaves like a Merkle tree: it compares external targets only through their hashes and ignores whether two targets are shared. Within a component, it inherits the equivalence realized by the component hash parameter. We make this precise for an arbitrary component hash. All relations in this subsection are defined on rooted vertices of arbitrary finite labeled multidigraphs (considered up to renaming of their vertex sets), rather than on the disjoint union of two fixed graphs. Thus they can be compared consistently across different inputs. For a vertex $u$, let $\Scc(u)$ be its SCC in its own graph, and let $\hgt(u)$ be the height of this SCC.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:merklegraphhash} passes the component hash the labels $L'(x)$, which contain hash values, whereas the equivalence we want to characterize is defined on the classes that these hashes stand for. We therefore constrain \textproc{comp\_hash} so that hash equality is unaffected by an injective relabeling of the input strongly connected component: two nodes have equal hashes before the relabeling if and only if they have equal hashes after it.

\begin{defn}[Component and Merkle equivalences]
\label{def:merkle-eq}
A \emph{component equivalence} is an equivalence relation $\sim$ on pairs $(S,x)$, where $S$ is a strongly connected labeled multidigraph and $x$ is one of its vertices. It is \emph{preserved and reflected by common injective relabeling}: for any two such components $S,S'$ and any injective map $f$ on the union of their label sets,
\[
(S,x)\sim(S',x')\iff(f(S),x)\sim(f(S'),x').
\]
Here $f(S)$ replaces each vertex label $\ell$ by $f(\ell)$. A component hash \emph{realizes} $\sim$ if, in the ideal hash model, $\textproc{comp\_hash}(S_1)[x_1]=\textproc{comp\_hash}(S_2)[x_2]$ if and only if $(S_1,x_1)\sim(S_2,x_2)$.

The \emph{Merkle equivalence} $\approx$ of $\sim$ is defined simultaneously across all finite labeled multidigraphs by induction on SCC height. At stage $h\geq 0$, assume $\approx_{h-1}$ is defined on vertices of height at most $h-1$ (the set is empty when $h=0$). For every vertex $x$ of height at most $h$, each external successor has height less than $h$. Let $X_h(x)$ be the multiset of the $\approx_{h-1}$-classes of its external successors, counted with multiplicity; it is empty when $h=0$. Let $C_h(x)$ be the sub-multigraph induced on $\Scc(x)$ with vertex labels $L_h(y)=(L(y),X_h(y))$. For vertices $u,v$ of height at most $h$, define
\[
u\approx_h v \iff (C_h(u),u)\sim(C_h(v),v).
\]
Each $\approx_h$ is an equivalence relation. Its restriction to vertices of height at most $h-1$ is $\approx_{h-1}$: on those components, the external-class symbols are changed only by a common injective renaming, under which $\sim$ is invariant. Consequently the relations $\approx_h$ are compatible and together define $\approx$ for arbitrary finite graphs. We use $X^{\approx}(x)$ and $C^{\approx}(x)$ for the external-class multiset and relabeled component obtained at stage $\hgt(x)$ (equivalently, at any later stage, up to an injective renaming of class symbols). In the set variant, $X_h(x)$ is a set instead of a multiset. We write $\eqg$ for the Merkle equivalence of rooted isomorphism.
\end{defn}

Rooted isomorphism, isomorphism of unfoldings and bisimilarity (Section~\ref{sec:local-graph-hashing}) are component equivalences. The recursion is well founded because external successors lie in SCCs of strictly smaller height. For these three component equivalences, $\approx$ moreover relates only nodes of equal height. Indeed, if $u\approx v$ then every node of $\Scc(u)$ corresponds to a node of $\Scc(v)$ with the same label $L^{\approx}$ and vice versa (for unfoldings and bisimilarity, because in a strongly connected graph every node is reachable from every other). So the two components have the same classes of external successors, and hence the same height.

\begin{theorem}[Correctness of the Merkle hashes]
\label{thm:merkle}
Let \textproc{comp\_hash} realize a component equivalence $\sim$, and let $\approx$ be the Merkle equivalence of $\sim$. Assuming $\mathcal{H}$ is injective, $\HM(G_1,\textproc{comp\_hash})[u]=\HM(G_2,\textproc{comp\_hash})[v]$ if and only if $u\approx v$. The same holds for the set variant.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Consider any two input graphs, with computed hashes $h_1,h_2$. We induct on $h=\max(\hgt(u),\hgt(v))$, establishing the claim for every pair at that height. When $h=0$ there are no external successors, so the labels passed to the component hash are exactly the component labels used to define $\approx_0$.

For $h>0$, the induction hypothesis applies to all pairs of vertices of height below $h$ in the two inputs, including pairs from the same input. Hence the map from an $\approx_{h-1}$-class of external successors to their common computed hash is well defined and injective on the classes represented in the two inputs. Crucially, this is \emph{one map shared across both graphs}, not a separately chosen map for each input.

Apply the map elementwise to the multisets $X_h(x)$. For every vertex $x$ of either compared component, its idealized label $(L(x),X_h(x))$ is thereby sent to the computed label $(L(x),X(x))$. On the union of the label sets of the two components this gives a single injective relabeling. By the common-relabeling property of $\sim$, the two computed components are $\sim$-equivalent at $u,v$ precisely when the components $C_h(u),C_h(v)$ are, i.e.\ precisely when $u\approx_h v$, or equivalently $u\approx v$. Since the component hash realizes $\sim$, this is equivalent to equality of the computed hashes. The same proof works with sets in place of multisets.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The global Merkle hash.} In the \emph{global variant} $\HMg$, for simple digraphs, the component hash is the global node hash (Algorithm~\ref{alg:digraph-node-hash}), which realizes rooted isomorphism by Theorem~\ref{thm:digraphnodehashcorrect}. The general multidigraph Merkle construction still applies with any component hash realizing the desired component equivalence, but the global component hash implemented here is specified only for simple digraphs. Theorem~\ref{thm:merkle} therefore gives the following.

\begin{cor}
\label{cor:merkle-global}
Assuming $\mathcal{H}$ is injective, $\HMg$ realizes $\eqg$ on labeled digraphs.
\end{cor}

Spelled out, $u\eqg v$ if and only if there is a bijection $\phi:\Scc(u)\to \Scc(v)$ with $\phi(u)=v$ that is a label-preserving isomorphism of the induced subgraphs and, for every $x\in \Scc(u)$, matches the external successors of $x$ bijectively with $\eqg$-equivalent external successors of $\phi(x)$. The global variant thus respects the full structure inside each component, including automorphisms and incoming edges inside the component. Proposition~\ref{prop:merkle-position} in Section~\ref{sec:local-merkle} places $\eqg$ between rooted isomorphism of reachable subgraphs and isomorphism of unfoldings.

\paragraph{Complexity and incrementality.} Computing the SCCs takes $O(n+m)$ time, and each component is relabeled and hashed once. The total combinatorial cost outside the component hashes is $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ for collecting and ordering external hash multisets, in addition to $\sum_S c(S)$, where $c(S)$ is the combinatorial cost of the component hash on $S$. Label preparation, serialization, and hashing are charged to $T_{\mathrm{lab}}+T_{\mathrm{ser}}$ (Section~\ref{sec:cost-model}). For the global variant, $c(S)$ is the cost of Algorithm~\ref{alg:digraph-node-hash} on $S$, which is GI-hard in general. The local variant of Section~\ref{sec:local-merkle} runs in $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ combinatorial time, and Section~\ref{sec:outgoing-ordered-edge-graphs} gives the costs for ordered graphs. As with Merkle trees, an edit can affect only the hashes of the modified SCC and components that can reach it (recomputing SCC membership when the edges change). Adding a new node whose edges point only to existing nodes creates a new singleton SCC and leaves all existing hashes unchanged. Merkle hashes deliberately forget whether subgraphs below a component are shared, exactly as Merkle trees and Merkle DAGs do in git and IPFS.

In comparison to the global hashing strategy which depends on the global structure of the graph, the local hashing strategy depends only on properties we can \textit{observe} by traversing the digraph forward along its edges. To make this concept precise, the hashes of two nodes should be equal if and only if their \textit{unfoldings} are isomorphic. We characterize this equivalence by stable partitions and merge equivalent nodes into a quotient (Section~\ref{sec:quotient}). Canonical color refinement \cite{berkholz2017tight} then orders the nodes of a strongly connected quotient, which gives a component hash (Sections~\ref{sec:canonical-refinement} and~\ref{sec:component-hash}). With this component hash, the Merkle hash of Section~\ref{sec:merkle-graph-hashing} realizes an SCC-annotated variant of unfolding isomorphism (Section~\ref{sec:local-merkle}), and applied to the quotient it realizes unfolding isomorphism itself (Section~\ref{sec:all-nodes}). The resulting hash has $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ combinatorial running time, plus label and serialization costs (Section~\ref{sec:cost-model}).

\subsection{Unfoldings.}
\begin{defn}[Unfolding]
%Let $G=(V,\mu,L)$ be a labeled multidigraph and $u\in V$. The \emph{unfolding} $\Unf(G,u)$ is the rooted, labeled, unordered tree whose nodes are the walks $w = (w_0, e_1, w_1, \dots, e_k, w_k)$ with $w_0 = u$, where $e_j \in \{1,\dots,\mu(w_{j-1},w_j)\}$ distinguishes parallel edges. The root is the walk $(u)$ of length $0$, the parent of a walk of length $k\ge 1$ is obtained by deleting its last step, and the label of $w$ is $L(w_k)$. The \emph{truncation} $\Unf_k(G,u)$ is the subtree of walks of length at most $k$.
Let $G=(V,\mu,L)$ be a labeled multidigraph and $u\in V$. The \emph{unfolding} $\Unf(G,u)$ is the rooted, labeled, unordered tree defined as follows. The nodes of $\Unf(G,u)$ are the walks in $G$ starting at $u$, i.e.\ sequences $w = (x_0, e_1, x_1, \dots, e_k, x_k)$ of vertices $x_j \in V$ of $G$ with $x_0 = u$ and $e_j \in \{1,\dots,\mu(x_{j-1},x_j)\}$ selecting one of the parallel edges from $x_{j-1}$ to $x_j$. Each walk, taken as a whole, is one tree node; the same vertex of $G$ can therefore occur in many tree nodes. The root is the walk $(u)$ of length $0$, the parent of a walk of length $k\ge 1$ is obtained by deleting its last step $(e_k, x_k)$, and the label of $w$ is $L(x_k)$, the label of the vertex at which the walk ends. The \emph{truncation} $\Unf_k(G,u)$ is the subtree of walks of length at most $k$.
\end{defn}

Isomorphism of rooted labeled unordered trees means a label-preserving bijection preserving the root and the parent relation. Two finite such trees are isomorphic if and only if the roots have equal labels and there is a bijection between the children of the two roots that maps each child subtree to an isomorphic child subtree. We use two elementary facts. First, the subtree of $\Unf(G,u)$ below a walk ending at $x$ is isomorphic to $\Unf(G,x)$. Second, the root of $\Unf(G,u)$ has exactly $\mu(u,x)$ children whose subtrees are copies of $\Unf(G,x)$, for each $x$. Unfoldings are infinite as soon as a cycle is reachable, but they are finitely branching. Figure \ref{fig:unfolding} shows an example. For nodes $u$ of $G_1$ and $v$ of $G_2$, which may be the same graph, we write $u\equiv v$ if $\Unf(G_1,u)\iso\Unf(G_2,v)$.

\begin{lemma}[Truncations suffice]
\label{lem:truncation}
$\Unf(G_1,u)\iso\Unf(G_2,v)$ if and only if $\Unf_k(G_1,u)\iso\Unf_k(G_2,v)$ for every $k\ge 0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
($\Rightarrow$) An isomorphism preserves depth, so it restricts to isomorphisms of the truncations.
($\Leftarrow$) Let $I_k$ be the set of isomorphisms $\Unf_k(G_1,u) \to \Unf_k(G_2,v)$. Each $I_k$ is finite (the trees are finite) and nonempty by assumption, and restriction maps $I_{k+1}$ into $I_k$. Let $J_k \subseteq I_k$ be the set of elements of $I_k$ that are restrictions of elements of $I_j$ for every $j > k$. Since the images of $I_j$ in $I_k$ form a decreasing chain of finite nonempty sets, $J_k$ is nonempty, and every element of $J_k$ is the restriction of an element of $J_{k+1}$. By K\H{o}nig's lemma \cite{konig1936theorie} (or simply by induction) there is a sequence $\phi_k \in J_k$ with $\phi_{k+1}$ extending $\phi_k$. Their union is an isomorphism $\Unf(G_1,u)\to\Unf(G_2,v)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Stable partitions and the unfolding quotient.}
\label{sec:quotient}
For a node $u$ and a set $Y$ of nodes of a labeled multidigraph $G=(V,\mu,L)$, let $\mu(u,Y)=\sum_{y\in Y}\mu(u,y)$ be the number of edges from $u$ into $Y$. A partition $P$ of $V$ is \emph{stable with respect to} $Y$ if $\mu(u,Y)=\mu(u',Y)$ for any two nodes $u,u'$ in the same block of $P$, and $P$ is \emph{stable} if the nodes in each block have equal labels and $P$ is stable with respect to each of its blocks. Stability with respect to $Y$ is preserved when $P$ is refined, and stability with respect to $D$ and to a subset $E\subseteq D$ implies stability with respect to $D\setminus E$, since $\mu(u,D\setminus E)=\mu(u,D)-\mu(u,E)$.

\begin{lemma}[Stable partitions]
\label{lem:stable}
The classes of $\equiv$ on $V$ form a stable partition, and every stable partition of $V$ refines it. Hence the classes of $\equiv$ form the coarsest stable partition of $V$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $u\equiv u'$, an isomorphism $\Unf(G,u)\to\Unf(G,u')$ preserves the label of the root and maps the children of the root bijectively onto the children of the root, and the subtree below each child, which is the unfolding of the end vertex of the corresponding edge, onto an isomorphic subtree. Hence $L(u)=L(u')$, and $u$ and $u'$ have the same number of edges into every class of $\equiv$.

Conversely, let $P$ be a stable partition. We show by induction on $k$ that $\Unf_k(G,u)\iso\Unf_k(G,u')$ for any two nodes $u,u'$ in the same block of $P$. For $k=0$ this holds because their labels are equal. For the step, $u$ and $u'$ have the same number of edges into every block $B$ of $P$. Bijections between these edges, for all blocks $B$, give a bijection between the children of the roots of $\Unf_{k+1}(G,u)$ and $\Unf_{k+1}(G,u')$ in which corresponding children are walks ending in the same block, and their depth-$k$ subtrees are isomorphic by the induction hypothesis. Hence $\Unf_{k+1}(G,u)\iso\Unf_{k+1}(G,u')$, and $u\equiv u'$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:truncation}.
\end{proof}

We write $[u]$ for the class of $u$, and $C:V\to V/{\equiv}$ for the map $u\mapsto[u]$.

\begin{defn}[Unfolding quotient]
\label{def:quotient}
We define the quotient multidigraph $Q = G/{\equiv}$, written $(V_Q,\mu_Q,L_Q)$, as follows: its vertices are the classes of $\equiv$, its labels are $L_Q([u]) = L(u)$, and its multiplicities are
\[
\mu_Q(A,B) = \sum_{x\in B}\mu(u,x) \quad\text{for any } u\in A,
\]
i.e.\ the number of edges from a single representative of $A$ into $B$.
\end{defn}

This is \emph{not} the naive quotient that creates one edge $[u]\to[v]$ for every edge $(u,v)$ of $G$: that construction multiplies multiplicities by class sizes and does not preserve unfoldings. Definition~\ref{def:quotient} coincides with the minimum base of a graph fibration \cite{boldi2002fibrations}. We call a labeled multidigraph \emph{reduced} if distinct vertices have non-isomorphic unfoldings.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:quotient}
The multiplicities $\mu_Q$ do not depend on the chosen representatives, and $\Unf(Q,[u])\iso\Unf(G,u)$ for every $u \in V$. The quotient $Q$ is reduced, so its coarsest stable partition is discrete. Finally, $C$ maps every edge of $G$ to an edge of $Q$, so $Q$ is strongly connected if $G$ is.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The first statement is the stability of $\equiv$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:stable}). Next we show $\Unf_k(Q,[u])\iso\Unf_k(G,u)$ by induction on $k$, simultaneously for all $u$. The case $k=0$ holds by the choice of $L_Q$. For the step, the root of $\Unf_{k+1}(G,u)$ has, for each class $B$, exactly $\mu_Q([u],B)$ children whose subtrees are $\Unf_k(G,x)$ with $x\in B$. By the induction hypothesis each of these is isomorphic to $\Unf_k(Q,B)$, and the root of $\Unf_{k+1}(Q,[u])$ has exactly $\mu_Q([u],B)$ children with subtree $\Unf_k(Q,B)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:truncation} gives the full unfoldings. If $A\ne B$ in $V_Q$ with representatives $u\not\equiv v$, then $\Unf(Q,A)\iso\Unf(G,u)\not\iso\Unf(G,v)\iso\Unf(Q,B)$, so $Q$ is reduced, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:stable} applied to $Q$ its coarsest stable partition is discrete. Finally, for an edge $(x,y)$ of $G$, choosing $x$ as the representative of $[x]$ in Definition~\ref{def:quotient} shows that $\mu_Q([x],[y])\ge\mu(x,y)>0$. So $C$ maps walks of $G$ to walks of $Q$, and since $C$ is surjective, $Q$ is strongly connected if $G$ is.
\end{proof}

In reduced graphs, isomorphic unfoldings come from isomorphic reachable subgraphs.

\begin{lemma}[Reduced graphs]
\label{lem:reduced-iso}
Let $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ be reduced labeled multidigraphs, $A\in V_{Q_1}$ and $B\in V_{Q_2}$. Then $A\equiv B$ if and only if there is an isomorphism between the sub-multigraphs induced on $\Reach(A)$ and $\Reach(B)$ that maps $A$ to $B$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
($\Leftarrow$) The unfolding of a vertex depends only on the sub-multigraph induced on its reachable set, and isomorphisms preserve unfoldings.

($\Rightarrow$) Let $\tau:\Unf(Q_1,A)\to\Unf(Q_2,B)$ be an isomorphism. Define $g:\Reach(A)\to\Reach(B)$ by letting $g(X)$ be the end vertex of $\tau(w)$ for any walk $w$ from $A$ to $X$. The subtree below $w$ is $\Unf(Q_1,X)$, and the subtree below $\tau(w)$ is $\Unf(Q_2,g(X))$, so $X\equiv g(X)$. Since $Q_2$ is reduced, $g(X)$ does not depend on the choice of $w$. The same argument with $\tau^{-1}$ gives an inverse map, so $g$ is a bijection with $g(A)=B$, and it preserves labels. For multiplicities, fix $X$ and $Z$ in $\Reach(A)$. Since $Q_1$ is reduced, the root of $\Unf(Q_1,X)$ has exactly $\mu_{Q_1}(X,Z)$ children whose subtrees are isomorphic to $\Unf(Q_1,Z)$. Since $Q_2$ is reduced and $Z\equiv g(Z)$, the root of $\Unf(Q_2,g(X))$ has exactly $\mu_{Q_2}(g(X),g(Z))$ children of this isomorphism type. An isomorphism between $\Unf(Q_1,X)$ and $\Unf(Q_2,g(X))$ preserves the number of children of each isomorphism type, so $\mu_{Q_1}(X,Z)=\mu_{Q_2}(g(X),g(Z))$. Thus $g$ is an isomorphism.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Canonical refinement.}
\label{sec:canonical-refinement}
The coarsest stable partition can be computed by partition refinement \cite{cardon1982partitioning,paige1987three}. We need a version whose result does not depend on how the nodes are named, so that its block numbers can serve as a canonical order. This is the \emph{canonical color refinement} of Berkholz, Bonsma and Grohe \cite{berkholz2017tight}. It maintains a partition into numbered blocks and a list of blocks to refine with. Refining with a block $E$ splits every block into groups of nodes with equal numbers of edges into $E$. The group with the smallest number keeps the number of the block, and the other groups receive new numbers in increasing order of their number of edges. As in Hopcroft's algorithm \cite{hopcraft}, a split block that is not in the list adds all of its groups to the list except a largest one, which gives the running time. Every decision depends only on block numbers, block sizes and numbers of edges, never on the names of the nodes. Our stable partitions are the stable colorings of \cite{berkholz2017tight}, which refine along outgoing edges, and for multidigraphs they are the edge-color-stable colorings with a single edge color, in which parallel edges are counted.

\begin{lemma}[Canonical refinement \cite{berkholz2017tight}]
\label{lem:canonical-refinement}
Let $G$ be a labeled multidigraph with $n$ nodes and $m$ explicitly represented edge occurrences. Start canonical color refinement from the partition into classes of nodes with equal labels, numbered in increasing order of their canonical encodings. It computes the coarsest stable partition, whose blocks are the classes of $\equiv$, in $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ combinatorial time, plus the cost of preparing and sorting the labels as described in Section~\ref{sec:cost-model}. Its block numbers are canonical: if $\psi:G_1\to G_2$ is an isomorphism, then for every node $x$ of $G_1$, the block of $\psi(x)$ in the result for $G_2$ has the same number as the block of $x$ in the result for $G_1$. In particular, if the coarsest stable partition of $G$ is discrete, the block numbers form a canonical order of $V$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Berkholz, Bonsma and Grohe show that their algorithm computes, within this time, a coloring whose partition is the coarsest stable partition that refines the initial one, for digraphs and for edge-colored digraphs with parallel edges. They also show that it is canonical: every isomorphism that preserves the initial coloring preserves the computed colors. A label-preserving isomorphism preserves the initial numbering, which depends only on the labels. By Lemma~\ref{lem:stable}, the blocks of the coarsest stable partition are the classes of $\equiv$.
\end{proof}

The frequently used simplified bound $O((n+m)\log n)$ applies to simple digraphs and to multidigraphs with $m$ polynomial in $n$, but we do not require that restriction for explicitly represented parallel edges.

\subsection{Hashing a strongly connected graph.}
\label{sec:component-hash}
In a strongly connected graph, every node reaches every other node, so a canonical order of the whole quotient depends only on what each node can observe. Algorithm~\ref{alg:local-component-hash} serializes the quotient once in this order, and hashes each node by its position.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
    \caption{Local Component Hash}\label{alg:local-component-hash}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Function{\textproc{local\_comp\_hash}}{$S=(V_S,\mu_S,L_S)$} \Comment{$S$ strongly connected}
        \State $C \gets$ the classes of $\equiv$ on $V_S$ \Comment{partition refinement, Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-refinement}}
        \State $Q=(V_Q,\mu_Q,L_Q) \gets S / C$ \Comment{Definition~\ref{def:quotient}; $C : V_S\to V_Q$ maps nodes to classes}
        \State $\kappa \gets$ the block numbers of the canonical refinement of $Q$ \Comment{a canonical order, Lemma~\ref{lem:comp-quotient}}
        \State labels $\gets$ $[L_Q(X)$ \textbf{for} $X\in V_Q$ \textbf{in increasing order of} $\kappa(X)]$
        \State edges $\gets$ \textproc{sorted}($[(\kappa(X), \kappa(Y), \mu_Q(X,Y)) \mid X,Y\in V_Q,\ \mu_Q(X,Y) > 0]$)
        \State $g \gets \mathcal{H}(\enc((\text{labels}, \text{edges})))$
        \State \Return $\{x \mapsto \mathcal{H}(\enc((\kappa(C(x)), g))) \mid x \in V_S\}$
    \EndFunction
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:comp-quotient}
Let $S$ be strongly connected and let $Q$ be its quotient. Then $Q$ is strongly connected, $\kappa$ is injective, and $Q$ has no automorphism other than the identity.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:quotient}, $Q$ is strongly connected and its coarsest stable partition is discrete, so $\kappa$ is injective by Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-refinement}. An automorphism of $Q$ maps every vertex to a vertex with an isomorphic unfolding, so it is the identity because $Q$ is reduced.
\end{proof}

In particular, no orbit computation is needed: the canonical index of a vertex of $Q$ identifies it uniquely.

\begin{theorem}[Correctness of the local component hash]
\label{thm:local-component}
Let $S_1,S_2$ be strongly connected labeled multidigraphs, let $x_1\in V_{S_1}$ and $x_2\in V_{S_2}$, and write $h_S=\textproc{local\_comp\_hash}(S)$. Assuming $\mathcal{H}$ is injective, $h_{S_1}(x_1)=h_{S_2}(x_2)$ if and only if $x_1\equiv x_2$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:local-component-hash} runs in $O((n+m)\log(n+m))+T_{\mathrm{lab}}+T_{\mathrm{ser}}$ time, where $n$ and $m$ are the numbers of vertices and explicit edge occurrences of $S$ and the auxiliary costs are as in Section~\ref{sec:cost-model}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $Q_1,Q_2$ be the quotients, $A=C_1(x_1)$ and $B=C_2(x_2)$.

($\Leftarrow$) By Lemma~\ref{lem:quotient}, $A\equiv B$. By Lemmas~\ref{lem:reduced-iso} and~\ref{lem:comp-quotient}, the reachable sets of $A$ and $B$ are all of $Q_1$ and $Q_2$, and there is an isomorphism $\psi:Q_1\to Q_2$ with $\psi(A)=B$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-refinement}, $\kappa_2(\psi(X))=\kappa_1(X)$ for every vertex $X$ of $Q_1$. Hence the label lists, the edge lists, the hashes $g$ and the indices of $A$ and $B$ coincide.

($\Rightarrow$) By injectivity, $\kappa_1(A)=\kappa_2(B)$, and the label and edge lists coincide. Since $\kappa_1$ and $\kappa_2$ are injective, mapping the vertex with $\kappa_1=i$ to the vertex with $\kappa_2=i$, for every $i$, is an isomorphism $Q_1\to Q_2$ that maps $A$ to $B$. Hence $A\equiv B$, and $x_1\equiv x_2$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:quotient}.

The running time follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-refinement}, applied to $S$ and to $Q$, together with $O(n+m)$ time for constructing the quotient from explicit edges and $O(m\log(n+m))$ word operations for sorting the edge records. The label and serialization costs are charged separately. In particular, multiplicities in $Q$ are at most $m$ and fit in one word in the stated model.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The local Merkle hash.}
\label{sec:local-merkle}
Isomorphism of unfoldings is a component equivalence, and by Theorem~\ref{thm:local-component} the local component hash realizes it. In the \emph{local variant} $\HMl$ of the Merkle hash, Algorithm~\ref{alg:merklegraphhash} uses the local component hash, and we write $\eql$ for the Merkle equivalence of isomorphism of unfoldings. Theorem~\ref{thm:merkle} gives the following.

\begin{cor}
\label{cor:merkle-local}
Assuming $\mathcal{H}$ is injective, $\HMl$ realizes $\eql$. It runs in $O((n+m)\log(n+m))+T_{\mathrm{lab}}+T_{\mathrm{ser}}$ time for a graph with $n$ nodes and $m$ explicit edge occurrences.
\end{cor}
\begin{proof}
The first statement is Theorem~\ref{thm:merkle}. A component $S$ with $n_S$ vertices and $m_S$ internal edge occurrences requires $O((n_S+m_S)\log(n_S+m_S+2))$ combinatorial time by Theorem~\ref{thm:local-component}. The components are disjoint, and every edge occurrence is internal to one component or leaves one. Adding the external-edge processing of Section~\ref{sec:merkle-graph-hashing} gives $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ combinatorial time overall. The auxiliary costs are accumulated over all component calls.
\end{proof}

The local equivalence has a direct description in terms of unfoldings. The \emph{SCC-annotated unfolding} $\UnfA(G,u)$ is the unfolding $\Unf(G,u)$ in which every tree edge, corresponding to a step from $x_{k-1}$ to $x_k$, is marked \emph{internal} if $x_k\in \Scc(x_{k-1})$ and \emph{external} otherwise. Isomorphisms of annotated unfoldings must preserve the marks.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:annotated}
For nodes $u$ of $G_1$ and $v$ of $G_2$, $u\eql v$ if and only if $\UnfA(G_1,u)\iso\UnfA(G_2,v)$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
Marks depend only on the endpoints of each step, so the subtree of $\UnfA(G,u)$ below a walk ending at $y$ is $\UnfA(G,y)$. The walks from $u$ that use only internal steps stay in $\Scc(u)$. They form a subtree, the \emph{internal part}, which is the unfolding at $u$ of the subgraph induced on $\Scc(u)$. A tree node of the internal part ending at $x$ has one internal child for each internal edge of $x$ and one external child for each external edge. We proceed by induction on $\max(\hgt(u),\hgt(v))$.

($\Rightarrow$) Let $\tau$ be an isomorphism of the unfoldings of $C^{\approx}(u)$ and $C^{\approx}(v)$, i.e.\ of the internal parts, preserving $L^{\approx}$. For a tree node $w$ ending at $x$ with $\tau(w)$ ending at $x'$, we have $L(x)=L(x')$ and $X^{\approx}(x)=X^{\approx}(x')$. So there is a bijection between the external successors of $x$ and those of $x'$ relating only $\eql$-equivalent nodes, whose annotated unfoldings are isomorphic by the induction hypothesis. Extending $\tau$ at every external child by these isomorphisms gives an isomorphism $\UnfA(G_1,u)\to\UnfA(G_2,v)$.

($\Leftarrow$) An isomorphism $\sigma$ of the annotated unfoldings maps internal children to internal children, so it restricts to an isomorphism $\tau$ of the internal parts preserving $L$. For a tree node $w$ ending at $x$ with $\sigma(w)$ ending at $x'$, $\sigma$ maps the external children of $w$ bijectively onto those of $\sigma(w)$, with isomorphic annotated subtrees. By the induction hypothesis the corresponding external successors are $\eql$-equivalent, so $X^{\approx}(x)=X^{\approx}(x')$. Thus $\tau$ preserves $L^{\approx}$, and $u\eql v$.
\end{proof}

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:merkle-position}
For nodes $u$ of $G_1$ and $v$ of $G_2$,
\[
(G_1[\Reach(u)],u)\iso(G_2[\Reach(v)],v) \;\Rightarrow\; u\eqg v \;\Rightarrow\; u\eql v \;\Rightarrow\; \Unf(G_1,u)\iso\Unf(G_2,v),
\]
and none of the converse implications holds.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
A rooted isomorphism of reachable subgraphs maps SCCs to SCCs, so it witnesses Definition~\ref{def:merkle-eq} for $\eqg$ at every level. For the second implication, proceed by induction on height. If $\phi$ is a rooted isomorphism witnessing $u\eqg v$, then $\phi$ preserves the labels $L^{\approx}$ defined with $\eql$ as well. This holds because, by the induction hypothesis, a matching of external successors by $\eqg$-equivalent nodes is also a matching by $\eql$-equivalent nodes. A rooted isomorphism induces an isomorphism of unfoldings, so $u\eql v$. The third implication follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:annotated} by forgetting the marks.

For strictness, all nodes in the following examples carry the same label.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{First implication.} Let $x\to p$, $x\to q$, $p\to z$ and $q\to z$, and compare with the same graph in which $z$ is replaced by two copies $z_1,z_2$ with $p\to z_1$ and $q\to z_2$. The nodes $x$ are $\eqg$-equivalent, since all SCCs are singletons and the external multisets agree, but the reachable subgraphs have $4$ and $5$ nodes.
  \item \emph{Second implication.} A node with a self-loop and a node on a $2$-cycle are $\eql$-equivalent, since both internal unfoldings are infinite paths without external edges. They are not $\eqg$-equivalent, since their SCCs have different sizes.
  \item \emph{Third implication.} A node $u$ with a self-loop, and a node $v$ whose only edge leads to a node $w$ with a self-loop, have isomorphic unfoldings (infinite paths). They are not $\eql$-equivalent, because the first step from $u$ is internal and the first step from $v$ is external.
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why the internal/external marks remain]
\label{rem:compositional}
In both variants, a node $x$ that lies on no cycle is hashed as $F(L(x),\text{successor hashes})$ for a function $F$ built from $\mathcal{H}$. No scheme of this kind can realize plain unfolding isomorphism. In the third example above, unfolding isomorphism would require $h(v)=h(w)$, while $h(v)=F(L,\mset{h(w)})$. So computing $h(w)$ would produce a fixed point of $y\mapsto F(L,\mset{y})$, which is infeasible if $\mathcal{H}$ behaves like a random function. The marks are thus the price of Merkle-style compositionality over the condensation of the graph itself. The local hash of Section~\ref{sec:all-nodes} realizes plain unfolding isomorphism by applying the same recursion to the condensation of the unfolding quotient instead, where $v$ and $w$ are the same vertex.
\end{remark}

The global variant retains the full structure inside each cycle; it suits applications that must distinguish, for example, a single self-referencing node from a cycle of identical nodes. The local variant retains only what can be observed by following edges, together with whether each edge leaves its component. It has $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ combinatorial running time plus label and serialization costs in every case, while the global variant can be much slower (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), it merges nodes that no traversal can tell apart, and it is the variant used in Section~\ref{sec:application}.

\subsection{Hashing all nodes.}
\label{sec:all-nodes}
If a graph is not strongly connected, the canonical order of its whole quotient also depends on vertices that a node cannot reach, so the hash of the node would not be determined by its unfolding. The local Merkle hash avoids this, but by Remark~\ref{rem:compositional} it also distinguishes nodes by the marks of their annotated unfoldings. We therefore apply it to the quotient, where the marks make no difference (Corollary~\ref{cor:quotient-merkle}). Algorithm~\ref{alg:local-node-hash} gives the pseudo-code.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
    \caption{Local Digraph Node Hash}\label{alg:local-node-hash}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Function{$\HL$}{$G=(V,\mu,L)$}
        \State $C \gets$ the classes of $\equiv$ on $V$ \Comment{partition refinement, Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-refinement}}
        \State $Q=(V_Q,\mu_Q,L_Q) \gets G / C$ \Comment{Definition~\ref{def:quotient}}
        \State $h \gets \HM(Q, \textproc{local\_comp\_hash})$ \Comment{Algorithm~\ref{alg:merklegraphhash}, i.e.\ $h=\HMl(Q)$}
        \State \Return $\{u \mapsto h[C(u)] \mid u \in V\}$
    \EndFunction
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{cor}
\label{cor:quotient-merkle}
For vertices $A$ and $B$ of unfolding quotients $Q_1$ and $Q_2$, the following are equivalent: $A\equiv B$; $A\eqg B$; and $A\eql B$. In particular, $\HMl(Q)$ realizes unfolding isomorphism, and so does $\HMg(Q)$ if $Q$ has no parallel edges.
\end{cor}
\begin{proof}
If $A\equiv B$, Lemmas~\ref{lem:quotient} and~\ref{lem:reduced-iso} give an isomorphism between the sub-multigraphs induced on $\Reach(A)$ and $\Reach(B)$ that maps $A$ to $B$, so $A\eqg B$ and $A\eql B$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:merkle-position}. Conversely, $A\eqg B$ implies $A\eql B$, which implies $A\equiv B$ by the same proposition. The last statement follows from Corollaries~\ref{cor:merkle-global} and~\ref{cor:merkle-local}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Correctness of the local hash]
\label{thm:localhashcorrect}
$\HL$ realizes unfolding isomorphism: in the ideal hash model, for nodes $u$ of $G_1$ and $v$ of $G_2$, $\HL(G_1)[u] = \HL(G_2)[v]$ if and only if $\Unf(G_1, u) \iso \Unf(G_2, v)$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:local-node-hash} runs in $O((n+m)\log(n+m))+T_{\mathrm{lab}}+T_{\mathrm{ser}}$ time for $n$ vertices and $m$ explicit edge occurrences. The combinatorial term includes partition refinement, quotient construction, and component processing; the auxiliary terms include the lengths of original and derived labels and all serialized records.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $Q_1,Q_2$ be the quotients. By Lemma~\ref{lem:quotient}, $\Unf(G_i,u)\iso\Unf(Q_i,C_i(u))$, and $\HL(G_i)[u]=\HMl(Q_i)[C_i(u)]$. By Corollary~\ref{cor:merkle-local}, $\HMl(Q_1)[A]=\HMl(Q_2)[B]$ if and only if $A\eql B$, which holds if and only if $A\equiv B$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:quotient-merkle}.

\emph{Running time.} Line~2 takes $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ combinatorial time by Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-refinement}. The quotient has at most $n$ vertices and $m$ explicit edge occurrences, and is constructed in $O(n+m)$ word operations. Line~4 takes $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ combinatorial time by Corollary~\ref{cor:merkle-local}. Its structured labels contain references to original labels and fixed-size successor hashes; copying or serializing variable-length labels is not charged as a constant-time operation but included in $T_{\mathrm{lab}}+T_{\mathrm{ser}}$. Each final node hash has fixed size for a concrete digest, but its input serialization may not.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Alternative formulations of local hashing.}
The local hash realizes what an observer can see by traversing the graph forward along its directed edges. We note two other formulations.

\paragraph{Walk languages.} Treat labels as letters of an alphabet and define the \emph{vertex language} of $u$ as the set of label sequences of walks starting at $u$:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Lang}_G(u) = \{L(u)\} \cup \bigcup_{v \in \Succ(u)} \{L(u) \circ x \mid x \in \text{Lang}_G(v)\}.
\end{equation}
This resembles a nondeterministic finite automaton in which letters are emitted when visiting nodes rather than when traversing edges, and in which every state is accepting. Every vertex language is therefore prefix-closed.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:pspace}
Deciding $\text{Lang}_G(u)=\text{Lang}_G(v)$ is PSPACE-complete. Consequently, unless $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{PSPACE}$, there is no polynomial-time computable function $h$ with $h(u)=h(v)$ if and only if $\text{Lang}_G(u)=\text{Lang}_G(v)$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
Membership: a vertex language is the language of an NFA with one state per vertex plus an initial state, and NFA equivalence is in PSPACE.
Hardness: we reduce from equivalence of NFAs in which all states are final, which is PSPACE-complete over alphabets of size at least two \cite{kao2009nfas}. Let $M=(P,\Sigma,\delta,p_0,P)$ be such an NFA without $\varepsilon$-moves. Build a vertex-labeled graph with a root $r$ labeled by a fresh letter $\#$ and one vertex $t$ labeled $a$ for each transition $t=(p,a,q)\in\delta$. Add edges $r \to t$ for every transition leaving $p_0$, and $t \to t'$ whenever $t=(p,a,q)$ and $t'$ leaves $q$. Walks from $r$ correspond exactly to runs of $M$ from $p_0$, and every run is accepting, so $\text{Lang}(r) = \{\#w \mid w\in L(M)\}$. The graph has $O(|\delta|^2)$ edges. Building the graphs for two such NFAs $M_1,M_2$ side by side gives roots with equal languages if and only if $L(M_1)=L(M_2)$. The final statement follows because $h$ would decide the problem in polynomial time.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Bisimulation.} Nodes $a$ and $b$ (possibly in different graphs) are \emph{bisimilar} if they are related by the largest relation $\mathcal{R}$ such that $a\mathcal{R}b$ implies: (a) $L(a)=L(b)$; (b) for every edge $a\to a'$ there is an edge $b\to b'$ with $a'\mathcal{R}b'$; and (c) for every edge $b\to b'$ there is an edge $a\to a'$ with $a'\mathcal{R}b'$ \cite{bisimulation_book}. The existential quantifiers, in place of a bijection between successors, mean that bisimulation ignores duplicated bisimilar successors: bisimilar nodes may have different out-degrees. Unfolding isomorphism is the counting analogue of bisimilarity and implies it.

A partition of $V$ is \emph{bisimulation-stable} if the nodes in each block have equal labels and, for any two blocks $B$ and $B'$, either every node of $B$ has a successor in $B'$ or none has. The bisimilarity classes form the coarsest bisimulation-stable partition: by clauses (b) and (c) bisimilar nodes have successors in the same classes, and conversely, the relation of lying in the same block of a bisimulation-stable partition is a bisimulation. The relational coarsest partition algorithm of Paige and Tarjan \cite{paige1987three} computes these classes in $O((n+m)\log n)$ combinatorial time after labels are initialized, see also \cite{kanellakis1990ccs}. The \emph{bisimulation quotient} has the classes as vertices, with the labels of their members, and an edge $A\to B$ if and only if some, equivalently every, node of $A$ has a successor in $B$. The pairs $(u,[u])$ form a bisimulation, so every node is bisimilar to its class, and by transitivity distinct vertices of a bisimulation quotient are not bisimilar.

The \emph{bisimulation hash} $\HB$ is Algorithm~\ref{alg:local-node-hash} with three changes, which apply to Algorithm~\ref{alg:local-component-hash} as well. The classes are the bisimilarity classes, and the quotients are bisimulation quotients. Algorithm~\ref{alg:merklegraphhash} is used in its set variant, in which the external hashes $X(x)$ form a set rather than a multiset. And the edges are serialized without multiplicities. The canonical order $\kappa$ is still computed by the canonical color refinement of Section~\ref{sec:canonical-refinement}: unfolding isomorphism implies bisimilarity, so no two vertices of a bisimulation quotient have isomorphic unfoldings, and its coarsest stable partition is discrete.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:bisimhash}
$\HB$ realizes bisimilarity: in the ideal hash model, $\HB(G_1)[u]=\HB(G_2)[v]$ if and only if $u$ and $v$ are bisimilar. It runs in $O((n+m)\log(n+m))+T_{\mathrm{lab}}+T_{\mathrm{ser}}$ time on explicitly represented edges.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We write $\sim$ for bisimilarity, and follow the proofs of Lemma~\ref{lem:reduced-iso}, Theorem~\ref{thm:local-component} and Corollary~\ref{cor:quotient-merkle}.

\emph{Bisimulation quotients.} Let $A$ and $B$ be vertices of bisimulation quotients $Q_1$ and $Q_2$. Then $A\sim B$ if and only if there is an isomorphism between the sub-digraphs induced on $\Reach(A)$ and $\Reach(B)$ that maps $A$ to $B$. Indeed, such an isomorphism is a bisimulation. Conversely, if $A\sim B$, then by induction along walks, using clauses (b) and (c), every vertex of $\Reach(A)$ is bisimilar to a vertex of $\Reach(B)$, and vice versa. Since distinct vertices of the same quotient are not bisimilar, $\sim$ relates $\Reach(A)$ and $\Reach(B)$ by a bijection $g$ with $g(A)=B$, which preserves labels. If $X\to Z$, then $g(X)$ has a successor bisimilar to $Z$, which can only be $g(Z)$; symmetrically, every edge $g(X)\to g(Z)$ comes from an edge $X\to Z$. So $g$ is an isomorphism.

\emph{Strongly connected graphs.} As in Lemma~\ref{lem:quotient}, the bisimulation quotient of a strongly connected graph is strongly connected, and $\kappa$ is injective on it. With the previous paragraph in place of Lemma~\ref{lem:reduced-iso}, the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:local-component} shows that the component hashes of $x_1$ and $x_2$ are equal if and only if $x_1\sim x_2$, since an isomorphism of quotients is a bisimulation, and $x_i\sim C_i(x_i)$.

\emph{All nodes.} Bisimilarity is a component equivalence, and by the previous paragraph the bisimulation component hash realizes it. By Theorem~\ref{thm:merkle} for the set variant, $h_1(A)=h_2(B)$ if and only if $A\approx B$, where $\approx$ is the Merkle equivalence of bisimilarity with sets. We show that $A\approx B$ if and only if $A\sim B$, for vertices $A$ of $Q_1$ and $B$ of $Q_2$, by induction on $\max(\hgt(A),\hgt(B))$. Let $K$ and $K'$ be the components of $A$ and $B$. ($\Leftarrow$) The isomorphism $\psi$ of the first paragraph maps $K$ onto $K'$, and the external successors of every $x\in K$ bijectively onto those of $\psi(x)$. They are bisimilar to their images and have smaller height, so they are $\approx$-equivalent to their images by the induction hypothesis. Hence $X^{\approx}(\psi(x))=X^{\approx}(x)$, and $\psi$ restricts to an isomorphism $C^{\approx}(A)\to C^{\approx}(B)$, which is a bisimulation relating $A$ and $B$. So $A\approx B$. ($\Rightarrow$) There is a bisimulation $R$ between $C^{\approx}(A)$ and $C^{\approx}(B)$ that relates $A$ and $B$ and respects the labels $L^{\approx}$. If $x\,R\,x'$, then $L(x)=L(x')$ and $X^{\approx}(x)=X^{\approx}(x')$, so by the induction hypothesis every external successor of $x$ is bisimilar to some external successor of $x'$, and vice versa; and every internal successor of either node is related by $R$ to an internal successor of the other. More explicitly, let $h=\max(\hgt(A),\hgt(B))$, and define $R'$ on the original quotient graphs to consist of the pairs of $R$, together with all bisimilar pairs of vertices whose heights are both strictly below $h$. If $x\,R\,x'$, matching internal successors are provided by $R$; for each external successor, equality of the external $\approx$-class sets provides an external successor on the other side in the same class, hence bisimilar by the induction hypothesis. Their pair belongs to $R'$. The same argument works in the reverse direction. Pairs in $R'$ of smaller height already satisfy the bisimulation conditions because bisimilarity is a bisimulation on the original graphs. Therefore $R'$ is a bisimulation relating $A$ to $B$, and $A\sim B$.

\emph{Running time.} The algorithm of Paige and Tarjan takes $O((n+m)\log n)$ combinatorial time on the explicit adjacency representation after label initialization, and the remaining bounds are as in Theorem~\ref{thm:localhashcorrect}; variable-length labels and serialization are accounted for separately.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Relation to prior work.}
Refinement by counting edges is color refinement, the 1-dimensional Weisfeiler--Leman algorithm \cite{weisfeiler1968reduction}, applied along outgoing edges; the Weisfeiler--Lehman subtree kernels \cite{Shervashidze2011-dt,fast_subtree_kernels_on_graphs} hash the colors of its rounds. Partition refinement for automata goes back to Hopcroft's DFA minimization algorithm \cite{hopcraft, hopcraft-redescribe}. The equivalence $\equiv$ and the quotient $Q$ correspond to the fibration-theoretic notions of \emph{views} and \emph{minimum bases} \cite{yamashita1996anonymous,boldi2002fibrations}, and for undirected graphs, isomorphism of unfoldings is isomorphism of universal covers \cite{norris1995universal}. The canonical color refinement of Berkholz, Bonsma and Grohe \cite{berkholz2017tight}, which we use, computes colorings that are preserved by isomorphisms of the whole graph; colors of nodes in non-isomorphic graphs cannot be compared, which is what the recursion over the components of the quotient achieves. Unfoldings of ordered graphs are regular trees, and unique, maximally shared representations and canonical forms of regular trees and of recursive types have been computed with Hopcroft's algorithm \cite{mauborgne2000incremental,considine2000efficient,gauthier2004numbering}; the ordered variant of Section~\ref{sec:outgoing-ordered-edge-graphs} is closely related to these. To our knowledge, hashes for unordered unfoldings and for bisimilarity that are canonical across graphs and can be computed in near-linear time have not been described before.

In many real world applications the outgoing edges of each node are ordered. Examples include the links of a web page, the fields of a record, and the arguments of a function call. We take advantage of this extra structure to create simpler and faster hashing algorithms. The algorithms in this section were inspired by the more general Scott graph canonization algorithm of Bloyet, Marteau and Fr\'enod \cite{scott}. The basic idea is that, because the edges are ordered, a depth-first search from a given root visits the nodes in an order that is determined by the structure alone.

\paragraph{Model.} An \emph{ordered labeled digraph} assigns to each node $u$ a label $L(u)$ and a finite \emph{out-sequence} $s(u)=(s_1(u),\dots,s_{d(u)}(u))$ of successor nodes; position $k$ is the $k$-th \emph{port} of $u$, and repeated entries (two links to the same page) are allowed. Forgetting the order, the entries of $s(u)$ form the multiset $\Succ(u)$ of Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries}, so an ordered graph is a labeled multidigraph with additional structure. An \emph{ordered isomorphism} $\phi:G_1\to G_2$ is a label-preserving bijection with $s(\phi(u)) = (\phi(s_1(u)),\dots,\phi(s_{d(u)}(u)))$ for all $u$. Ordered automorphisms, orbits and rooted ordered isomorphisms are defined accordingly. For a node $n$ we write $G[\Reach(n)]$ for the ordered subgraph induced on its reachable set.

\begin{exmp}[Edge order must be serialized]
\label{ex:ordered}
Sorting the relabeled adjacency list, as an earlier version of this algorithm did, loses the port order, and the depth-first numbering does not always recover it because edges to already-visited nodes do not affect the numbering. Take $s(r)=(x,y)$, $s(y)=()$, and either $s(x)=(y,r)$ or $s(x)=(r,y)$. In both graphs the search from $r$ numbers $r,x,y$ as $0,1,2$, and the sorted edge set is $\{(0,1),(0,2),(1,0),(1,2)\}$ in both cases, even though the two graphs are not ordered-isomorphic.
\end{exmp}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
    \caption{Node Hash --- Outgoing Ordered Edges}
    \label{alg:node-outgoing-edges}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item From the input node $n$, perform a depth-first search that explores the out-edges of each node in port order, and number the reachable nodes $0,1,2,\dots$ in order of discovery (preorder).
        \item Create the list of original labels in discovery order.
        \item For each node in discovery order, write its out-sequence as a sequence of discovery numbers, \emph{keeping the port order} (do not sort).
        \item Return $\HO(G,n)=\mathcal{H}(\enc((\text{label list},\ \text{out-sequence list})))$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:orderedhash}
$\HO$ realizes rooted ordered isomorphism of reachable subgraphs: in the ideal hash model, $\HO(G_1,n_1)=\HO(G_2,n_2)$ if and only if there is an ordered isomorphism on the reachable subgraphs $G_1[\Reach(n_1)]\to G_2[\Reach(n_2)]$ mapping $n_1$ to $n_2$. Writing $r=|\Reach(n)|$ and $e=\sum_{x\in\Reach(n)}d(x)$ for the number of reachable vertices and their out-sequence entries, it takes $O(r+e)$ traversal and indexing operations, plus the cost of reading the labels and serializing and hashing the rooted code (Section~\ref{sec:cost-model}).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
($\Leftarrow$) The depth-first search is deterministic given the port orders. An ordered isomorphism $\psi$ commutes with every step of it: by induction on the steps, the searches from $n_1$ and $\psi(n_1)=n_2$ discover $x$ and $\psi(x)$ at the same step. Hence $\psi$ preserves discovery numbers, and the serializations coincide.
($\Rightarrow$) Equal serializations have equal lengths. Map the $k$-th discovered node of $G_1$ to the $k$-th discovered node of $G_2$. The label lists agree, and the out-sequences of corresponding nodes are equal as sequences of discovery numbers, so the map is an ordered isomorphism mapping $n_1$ (number $0$) to $n_2$.
\end{proof}

$\HO$ is a hash of the reachable subgraph, not of the unfolding. For example, a node with a self-loop and a node on a 2-cycle (with all labels equal) have isomorphic unfoldings but different values of $\HO$. The unfolding-based notion for ordered graphs uses \emph{ordered unfoldings}, in which the children of every tree node are ordered by port, and isomorphisms must preserve this order; in this section we write $u\equiv v$ if the ordered unfoldings of $u$ and $v$ are isomorphic. An ordered graph is a deterministic automaton whose letters are the ports and whose output at each state is its label and out-degree, and the classes of $\equiv$ are the states of the minimal automaton.

\paragraph{Merkle hashing of ordered graphs.} For ordered graphs, Line~\ref{line:merkle-X} of Algorithm~\ref{alg:merklegraphhash} becomes
\[
X(x) \gets \big[\,(h[y]~\textbf{if}~y\notin S~\textbf{else}~\star)~\textbf{for}~y~\textbf{in}~s(x)\,\big],
\]
so that every node $x$ of a component $S$ is relabeled with the sequence whose $k$-th entry is $h[s_k(x)]$ if $s_k(x)\notin S$ and a \emph{placeholder} $\star$ otherwise, and the positions of internal and external links are both recorded. In Line~\ref{line:merkle-G}, $G'$ keeps the internal targets of each node in port order, and we encode $\star$ as a value that differs from every hash. Correspondingly, component equivalences are defined on strongly connected ordered graphs, and in Definition~\ref{def:merkle-eq}, $X^{\approx}(x)$ is the sequence whose $k$-th entry is the $\approx$-class of $s_k(x)$ if $s_k(x)\notin\Scc(x)$ and $\star$ otherwise, and $C^{\approx}(x)$ keeps the internal targets in port order. Theorem~\ref{thm:merkle} holds with the same proof, because $L'$ and the internal targets in port order determine every port, exactly as $C^{\approx}$ does. Rooted ordered isomorphism and isomorphism of ordered unfoldings are component equivalences, and we write $\eqg$ and $\eql$ for their Merkle equivalences; in the description of $\eqg$ after Corollary~\ref{cor:merkle-global}, the external successors are matched port by port, with internal ports mapped by $\phi$. Propositions~\ref{prop:annotated} and~\ref{prop:merkle-position} hold for ordered graphs with the same proofs and examples, with annotated unfoldings ordered by port and external successors matched port by port. A further example inside a single strongly connected graph is $s(a)=(b,a)$, $s(b)=(a,a)$ with equal labels. Here $a\eql b$, since all nodes have the same label and out-degree, but $a$ and $b$ lie in different orbits, since an automorphism exchanging them would map $s(a)=(b,a)$ to $(a,b)\ne s(b)$.

Section~\ref{sec:local-graph-hashing} carries over to ordered graphs as follows.

\begin{theorem}[Ordered local hashing]
\label{thm:ordered-local}
Change Section~\ref{sec:local-graph-hashing} as follows. A partition is \emph{stable with respect to} a set $Y$ if, for any two nodes $u,u'$ in the same block and every port $k$, $s_k(u)\in Y$ if and only if $s_k(u')\in Y$; and a stable partition must have equal labels and equal out-degrees in each block. The quotient has the out-sequences $s([u])=([s_1(u)],\dots,[s_{d(u)}(u)])$. The canonical refinement is canonical color refinement of the edge-colored digraph in which the edge from $u$ to $s_k(u)$ has color $k$ \cite{berkholz2017tight}, started from the classes of nodes with equal label and out-degree. Algorithm~\ref{alg:local-component-hash} serializes the out-sequence $(\kappa(s_1(X)),\dots,\kappa(s_{d(X)}(X)))$ of every vertex $X$ in place of the multiplicities. Finally, Algorithm~\ref{alg:local-node-hash} uses the ordered Merkle hash described above. With these changes, Lemmas~\ref{lem:stable}--\ref{lem:comp-quotient}, Theorems~\ref{thm:local-component} and~\ref{thm:localhashcorrect}, and Corollaries~\ref{cor:merkle-local} and~\ref{cor:quotient-merkle} hold for ordered unfoldings and ordered isomorphisms. In particular, the local hash realizes isomorphism of ordered unfoldings and runs in $O((n+m)\log(n+m))+T_{\mathrm{lab}}+T_{\mathrm{ser}}$ time for explicitly represented out-sequences, with $m$ counting their total entries.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We list the changes to the proofs. In Lemma~\ref{lem:stable}, an isomorphism of ordered unfoldings maps the $k$-th child of the root to the $k$-th child of the root, so equivalent nodes have equal out-degrees and equivalent $k$-th successors; conversely, in the induction, the $k$-th children of the two roots are matched with each other. In Lemma~\ref{lem:quotient}, the out-sequences are well defined by stability, and the remaining statements are proved in the same way. In Lemma~\ref{lem:reduced-iso}, an isomorphism $\tau$ of ordered unfoldings maps the walk from $A$ that follows the ports $k_1,\dots,k_j$ to the walk from $B$ that follows the same ports. Let $g(X)$ be the end vertex of $\tau(w)$ for any walk $w$ from $A$ to $X$. Since $Q_2$ is reduced, $g(X)$ does not depend on $w$, and by symmetry $g$ is a bijection. It preserves labels, and $s_k(g(X))=g(s_k(X))$, because $\tau$ maps the extension of $w$ by port $k$ to the extension of $\tau(w)$ by port $k$. Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-refinement} holds because a partition is stable in the ordered sense if and only if it is edge-color-stable for these colors: a node $u$ has one edge of color $k$ into a block $Y$ if $s_k(u)\in Y$, and none otherwise. The proofs of Lemma~\ref{lem:comp-quotient} and Theorem~\ref{thm:local-component} are unchanged, with out-sequences in place of multiplicities. Corollaries~\ref{cor:merkle-local} and~\ref{cor:quotient-merkle} and Theorem~\ref{thm:localhashcorrect} then follow as before from the ordered versions of Theorem~\ref{thm:merkle} and Proposition~\ref{prop:merkle-position}.
\end{proof}

In practice, the classes can be computed directly as the states of the minimal automaton with the algorithms of Hopcroft and of Valmari and Lehtinen \cite{hopcraft,valmari2008partial}, in $O((n+m)\log n)$ combinatorial time after label initialization, as the dihash library does.

\paragraph{Strongly connected graphs.} For the special case of a strongly connected ordered graph, the node hashes of Algorithm~\ref{alg:node-outgoing-edges} yield both a canonical form and the orbit partition. This is the case needed by the global variant of the Merkle hash, which hashes strongly connected components individually. (The local variant uses the ordered version of Algorithm~\ref{alg:local-component-hash} instead, which serializes the quotient of each component once.) A search from every node would take $O(n(n+m))$ time. It suffices, however, to search from the nodes of one class of $\equiv$, provided that the class is chosen in a way that isomorphisms respect, and the canonical refinement provides such a choice.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
    \caption{Canonization and Orbit Partitioning for Strongly Connected Ordered Graphs}
    \label{alg:scc-outgoing-edges}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Compute the classes of $\equiv$ with their canonical block numbers by the canonical refinement of Theorem~\ref{thm:ordered-local}. Let $C$ be a smallest class, and among the smallest classes the one with the least number.
        \item For each $r\in C$, compute the rooted label list and ordered out-sequence list in Steps~1--3 of Algorithm~\ref{alg:node-outgoing-edges}, \emph{before applying the hash}. Compare their injective encodings $\enc((\text{labels},\text{out-sequences}))$ lexicographically. Let $T\subseteq C$ consist of the roots with the least encoding, and choose any $r_0\in T$. Its discovery order is the canonical labeling $\rho$; the corresponding pair of lists is the canonical form $\canon(G)$.
        \item For $t\in T$, let $\sigma_t$ map the $i$-th node discovered from $r_0$ to the $i$-th node discovered from $t$. The orbit of $v$ is $\{\sigma_t(v)\mid t\in T\}$.
        \item The graph hash is $\mathcal{H}(\enc(\canon(G)))$, and the orbit index of $v$ is the least value of $\rho$ on the orbit of $v$. These are used exactly as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:digraph-node-hash}.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:scc-ordered}
Let $G_1,G_2$ be strongly connected ordered digraphs, and work in the ideal hash model. For parts~(c) and~(e), let $G$ denote an individual strongly connected ordered digraph with $n$ vertices and $m$ entries in its out-sequences. Then:
(a) $\HO(G_1,u)=\HO(G_2,v)$ if and only if there is an ordered isomorphism $\phi:G_1\to G_2$ with $\phi(u)=v$;
(b) $\canon(G_1)=\canon(G_2)$ if and only if $G_1\iso G_2$, and $\rho$ is an isomorphism onto $\canon(G)$;
(c) every ordered automorphism is determined by the image of a single node, so $|\Aut(G)|\le n$ and all orbits have equal size; the maps $\sigma_t$ of Step~3 are exactly the automorphisms of $G$, so Step~3 computes the orbits;
(d) the node hash of Step~4 realizes rooted ordered isomorphism, as in Theorem~\ref{thm:digraphnodehashcorrect};
(e) Algorithm~\ref{alg:scc-outgoing-edges} runs in $O((n+m)\log(n+m)+c\,(n+m))+T_{\mathrm{lab}}+T_{\mathrm{ser}}$ time, where $c=|C|$ and $T_{\mathrm{ser}}$ includes comparisons of the $c$ candidate rooted encodings. In particular, if some class of $\equiv$ has a single vertex, its combinatorial time is $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$; in the worst case it is $O(n(n+m))$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) Since $G_i$ is strongly connected, $\Reach(u)=V_1$ and $\Reach(v)=V_2$, so this is Theorem~\ref{thm:orderedhash}.
(b) Let $\phi : G_1 \to G_2$ be an isomorphism. By the canonicity of the block numbers (Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-refinement}, which holds for ordered graphs by Theorem~\ref{thm:ordered-local}), $\phi$ maps the class of $\equiv$ with number $i$ onto the class with number $i$, so it preserves the sizes and numbers of the classes, and $\phi(C_1)=C_2$. Because ordered isomorphisms preserve the depth-first discovery order, $\phi$ maps roots of $C_1$ to roots of $C_2$ having equal rooted serializations. Their sets of encodings therefore agree, so the lexicographically least encodings coincide. By injectivity of $\enc$, all minimizing roots yield the same pair of lists, independently of the string hash. Conversely, equal serializations yield an isomorphism as in Theorem~\ref{thm:orderedhash}. The discovery order is exactly the bijection onto the serialized graph.
(c) An automorphism commutes with the depth-first search from any node $u$, which reaches all nodes, so it is determined by $\sigma(u)$. Hence $\Aut(G)$ acts semiregularly. For $t\in T$, (a) gives an automorphism that maps $r_0$ to $t$. It commutes with the searches, so it maps the $i$-th node discovered from $r_0$ to the $i$-th node discovered from $t$, and it is $\sigma_t$. Conversely, an automorphism $\sigma$ maps $C$ onto itself by the argument of (b) with $G_1=G_2$, and the rooted serializations at $\sigma(r_0)$ and $r_0$ agree because the depth-first traversal is invariant under ordered isomorphism. Hence $\sigma(r_0)\in T$, and $\sigma=\sigma_{\sigma(r_0)}$ because both are determined by the image of $r_0$.
(d) With (b) in place of the canonizer, the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:digraphnodehashcorrect} applies verbatim; the orbit index of Step~4 is the minimum canonical index over the orbit by (c).
(e) Step~1 takes $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ combinatorial time by Theorem~\ref{thm:ordered-local}. Step~2 runs $c$ searches, each taking $O(n+m)$ traversal and indexing operations; generating and comparing their rooted encodings is charged to $T_{\mathrm{ser}}$, including repeated label text. Step~3 takes $O(|T|\,n)$ time, where $|T|\le c$. Costs of preparing labels are charged to $T_{\mathrm{lab}}$.
\end{proof}

In the dihash library, Step~1 uses the canonical Hopcroft refinement of the ordered local hash. On the largest strongly connected components of the random graphs of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, about $93\%$ of the nodes are alone in their class of $\equiv$, so $c=1$ and a single search suffices.

By Theorem~\ref{thm:scc-ordered}(d), the node hash of Algorithm~\ref{alg:scc-outgoing-edges} realizes rooted ordered isomorphism. With it as the component hash, the global Merkle hash $\HMg$ of ordered graphs therefore realizes $\eqg$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:merkle}, and a component $S$ takes $O((|S|+m_S)\log(|S|+m_S)+c_S(|S|+m_S))$ combinatorial time, plus its label and serialization costs, where $c_S\le|S|$ is the size of the class $C$ chosen in the relabeled component.

\paragraph{The worst case.} The bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:scc-ordered}(e) is quadratic when every class of $\equiv$ is large, and refining along incoming edges as well does not remove this case. Suppose that all nodes have the same label and the same out-degree, and that for every port $k$ every node is the $k$-th successor of exactly one node. Then every port permutes the nodes, no refinement along outgoing or incoming edges separates any two nodes, and $G$ is the Schreier graph of the transitive permutation group $\Gamma$ generated by the ports. The ordered automorphisms of $G$ are the permutations that commute with every port, i.e.\ the centralizer of $\Gamma$ in the symmetric group, and the orbit of $r$ is the set of fixed points of the stabilizer of $r$ in $\Gamma$. If there is a single port, $G$ is a cycle, and the least rotation of a cyclic string can be computed in linear time \cite{booth1980lexicographically}. That method uses the fact that every rotation of a string is a substring of the string written twice, which has no analogue for depth-first searches in general graphs. We do not know a subquadratic algorithm for the general case. The canonical code of Jiang and Bunke for ordered undirected graphs, in which the edges at each node are cyclically ordered, is likewise the least of $2m$ codes, one per starting edge, and is computed in $O(m^2)$ time \cite{jiang1998coding,jiang1999optimal}.

\paragraph{Why only strongly connected graphs?} For a general digraph, the orbit of a node depends not only on the structure of its reachable subgraph but also on its ancestors. Incoming edges can break the symmetry of an orbit, as demonstrated in Figure \ref{fig:breaksymmetry}, so the reachable-subgraph hashes of Algorithm~\ref{alg:node-outgoing-edges} can be equal for nodes in different orbits. In a strongly connected graph this cannot happen, because every node reaches every other node. For general ordered digraphs, canonization is as hard as for digraphs (Proposition~\ref{prop:ordered-gi}), so a polynomial-time algorithm would also give one for graph isomorphism. The Merkle construction of Section~\ref{sec:merkle-graph-hashing} only needs strongly connected components.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:ordered-gi}
Isomorphism of ordered digraphs is at least as hard as isomorphism of digraphs, even for acyclic ordered digraphs in which every node has out-degree $0$ or $2$. A canonization of such ordered digraphs yields a canonization of digraphs with linear overhead.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Given a digraph $D$, possibly with parallel edges, let $D'$ be the ordered digraph with a node for every vertex of $D$, labeled $\mathsf{v}$ and with out-degree $0$, and a node for every edge $e=(a,b)$ of $D$, labeled $\mathsf{e}$ and with out-sequence $s(e)=(a,b)$. It is acyclic, and it is built in linear time. An ordered isomorphism $\phi:D_1'\to D_2'$ preserves labels, so it maps vertices to vertices and edge nodes to edge nodes, and $s(\phi(e))=(\phi(a),\phi(b))$ for every edge $e=(a,b)$. Hence the restriction of $\phi$ to the vertices is a bijection that maps the edges from $a$ to $b$ bijectively to the edges from $\phi(a)$ to $\phi(b)$, i.e.\ an isomorphism $D_1\to D_2$. Conversely, an isomorphism $\psi:D_1\to D_2$, together with a bijection between the edges from $a$ to $b$ and those from $\psi(a)$ to $\psi(b)$ for every pair $(a,b)$, is an ordered isomorphism $D_1'\to D_2'$. Thus $D_1\iso D_2$ if and only if $D_1'\iso D_2'$, and a canonical form of $D'$ is a canonical form of $D$.
\end{proof}

Booth \cite{booth1978isomorphism} showed that isomorphism of finite automata is polynomial-time equivalent to graph isomorphism.

From the algorithms given so far, it may not be obvious how to implement these graph theoretic procedures for real world data. Here we show how to do so for a static hyperlinked web page system stored in a content addressed distributed hash table (DHT).

In this sketch, we assume that web pages are static HTML files that link to each other and to external pages, and that external pages are themselves published in the same content-addressed way. (An external page that links back into our site would belong to the same SCC and be hashed together with it.) The pages form an ordered digraph in which each page is a node, the links are edges, and the out-edges of a node are ordered by their position in the HTML file. Figure \ref{fig:webpage-example} gives a concrete example of the process described here.

The label $L(x)$ of a page is its HTML text with every link target replaced by a hole, and its out-sequence $s(x)$ lists the link targets in the order in which they appear (Row 2 of Figure~\ref{fig:webpage-example}). We compute the SCCs of the link graph and process them sinks first with Algorithm~\ref{alg:merklegraphhash}, in its version for ordered graphs (Section~\ref{sec:outgoing-ordered-edge-graphs}). Each page is relabeled with $L'(x)=(L(x),X(x))$. Here $X(x)$ has one entry per hole: the placeholder $\star$ for a link to a page of the same SCC, and the recursively computed hash of the target for a link leaving the SCC (Row 3). Because $L'(x)$ is not hashed, the page text and its external links appear verbatim in it, and its internal links are the edges of $G'$ in port order. We use the local Merkle hash of Section~\ref{sec:local-merkle}, in its ordered version (Section~\ref{sec:outgoing-ordered-edge-graphs}). For each SCC, Algorithm~\ref{alg:local-component-hash} computes the ordered quotient, which merges pages that cannot be told apart by following links, together with its canonical order. We then insert the following items into the distributed hash table:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The strongly connected component as a whole: \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Value}: the pair (labels, edges) of Algorithm~\ref{alg:local-component-hash}, i.e.\ the labels $L'$ of the quotient in canonical order, together with the internal out-sequence of each quotient node in canonical indices.
    \item \textbf{Key}: the string hash $\mathcal{H}$ of the value, which is exactly the component hash $g$ of Algorithm~\ref{alg:local-component-hash}.
  \end{itemize}
  \item For each page $x$, a reference to a specific node of the quotient: \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Value}: the pair $(\text{index}[C(x)], g)$ of the page's canonical index and the key of its SCC.
    \item \textbf{Key}: the string hash $\mathcal{H}$ of the value, which is exactly the Merkle hash $h[x]$ of the page.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

By Theorem~\ref{thm:merkle} and Proposition~\ref{prop:annotated}, two pages receive the same key exactly when their SCC-annotated ordered unfoldings are isomorphic. In other words, no sequence of clicks can tell them apart, except by observing which links leave their SCC. In particular, pages in the same orbit share a key, and so do duplicated pages. This is intended: the store is content-addressed, and such pages are indistinguishable by navigation. These hashes encode observable page content and navigation behavior, not distinct URL or document identity; applications requiring separate identities, access-control information, or independently changing metadata must store that information separately. No orbit computation is needed, because quotients of strongly connected graphs have no non-trivial automorphisms (Lemma~\ref{lem:comp-quotient}). The whole site is hashed in near-linear time in practice (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}). If instead the exact page structure of every SCC must be preserved, the global variant can be used: Algorithm~\ref{alg:scc-outgoing-edges} replaces the quotient by the full SCC with its orbit partition, and only pages in the same orbit share a key.

Suppose that a user wants to navigate our website and has obtained the hash of one specific page. The browser retrieves the pair $(i,g)$ stored under this hash, and then the value stored under $g$, which describes the whole quotient. To display quotient node $i$, the browser fills the holes of its label $L(x)$ in order. A hole whose entry in $X(x)$ is a hash receives that hash, which is the key of an external page. The $j$-th hole whose entry is $\star$ receives the $j$-th internal edge of node $i$, which is the canonical index of a node of the same quotient. If the user clicks a link within the same SCC, the browser simply displays the corresponding node of the quotient it already holds. Since the quotient preserves ordered unfoldings (Lemma~\ref{lem:quotient} and Theorem~\ref{thm:ordered-local}), the user sees exactly the sequence of pages they would see on the original site. For links that leave the SCC, the browser repeats the process from the beginning with the embedded key. Everything needed to display and navigate the site is thus contained in the hashed values themselves. As with any Merkle structure, an edit can affect only the hashes of the modified SCC and components that can reach it (the SCC decomposition may itself change following an edge edit).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Merkle hashing of cyclic definitions.} The core idea of the digraph Merkle hashing algorithm was independently discovered by the developers of the Unison programming language \cite{unison}, who hash the abstract syntax trees of mutually recursive definitions. Each function and datatype is a node in a digraph, with its AST as the node label, and each cycle of mutually recursive definitions is hashed as a unit. To our knowledge their scheme does not treat non-trivial automorphism orbits inside a cycle, which arise, for example, from two structurally identical mutually recursive definitions that differ only in name. Such orbits appear to be rare in practice, but a complete treatment requires either the orbit index of Algorithm~\ref{alg:digraph-node-hash} or the local Merkle hash of Section~\ref{sec:local-merkle}, in which non-trivial orbits never arise (Lemma~\ref{lem:comp-quotient}). Hash-consing of cyclic structures and unique representations of regular trees \cite{mauborgne2000incremental,considine2000efficient}, canonical forms of recursive types \cite{gauthier2004numbering}, and hashing of terms modulo $\alpha$-equivalence \cite{maziarz2021hashing} address closely related problems in programming language implementation.

\paragraph{Color refinement and Weisfeiler--Leman.} The Weisfeiler--Lehman graph hash \cite{Shervashidze2011-dt} iteratively hashes and aggregates the neighborhoods of each node of an undirected graph. Isomorphic graphs are guaranteed to receive equal hashes, but the converse fails: 1-dimensional Weisfeiler--Leman refinement \cite{weisfeiler1968reduction} cannot distinguish, for example, two disjoint triangles from a $6$-cycle, or any two regular graphs of the same order and degree. In the kernel literature the number of iterations $h$ is a parameter that controls the depth of the subtree features. When refinement is used to test or hash equivalence, as here, no parameter is needed, because refinement stops at the coarsest stable partition after at most $n-1$ splits. Efficient implementations of color refinement run in $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ combinatorial time on explicitly represented edge occurrences, which simplifies to $O((n+m)\log n)$ when $m$ is polynomial in $n$, excluding label-processing costs \cite{cardon1982partitioning,berkholz2017tight}. The local hash of Section \ref{sec:local-graph-hashing} refines along outgoing edges with the canonical color refinement of \cite{berkholz2017tight}, and its hashes can be compared across graphs. The equivalence it computes is the view equivalence of anonymous networks \cite{yamashita1996anonymous} and the fibration equivalence of Boldi and Vigna \cite{boldi2002fibrations}, and for undirected graphs it corresponds to isomorphism of universal covers \cite{norris1995universal}.

\paragraph{Bisimulation and automata.} Bisimilarity is computed by relational coarsest partition algorithms \cite{kanellakis1990ccs,paige1987three}, and state equivalence of deterministic automata by Hopcroft-style minimization \cite{hopcraft,hopcraft-redescribe,valmari2008partial}. Sections~\ref{sec:local-graph-hashing}--\ref{sec:merkle-graph-hashing} use these as the first stage of canonical hashing.

\paragraph{Canonization.} Canonical labeling tools \cite{nauty,mckay2014practical,bliss,saucy} and Babai's quasipolynomial-time isomorphism test and canonization \cite{babai2016quasipolynomial,babai2019canonical} underlie Section~\ref{sec:global-graph-hashing}. For ordered undirected graphs, in which the edges at each node are cyclically ordered, Jiang and Bunke \cite{jiang1998coding,jiang1999optimal} give a canonical code computed in $O(m^2)$ time from every starting edge, and Booth \cite{booth1978isomorphism} showed that isomorphism of finite automata is as hard as graph isomorphism (Section~\ref{sec:outgoing-ordered-edge-graphs}).

\paragraph{Arshad et al.} In a 2014 paper, Arshad et al. \cite{Arshad2014-cc} presented an algorithm for hashing directed graphs given additional inputs: a fixed source node and a depth-first search traversal tree. The traversal tree classifies the edges as tree, back, forward, and cross edges depending on search order, and the hash uses the traversal numbers to order the nodes. The authors note that isomorphic graphs may hash to different values because of this dependence on the source node and DFS tree. Their approach can be used to hash nodes with ordered outgoing edges and can be viewed as an alternative to Algorithm~\ref{alg:node-outgoing-edges}.

In a separate 2018 paper, Arshad et al. \cite{Arshad2018-ij} presented a graph hashing algorithm in which the hash of a node combines, by XOR, the hashes of the labels of all of that node's descendants; the graph hash is then computed by hashing each node together with an accumulated graph hash. As described, this approach has two weaknesses. First, XOR aggregation is not a multiset hash: an even number of identically labeled descendants cancels out, so a node with two identically labeled descendants contributes as if it had none. Second, the aggregation ignores the edge structure among the descendants. For example, in the directed $k$-cycle and in the complete digraph on $k$ vertices, with all labels equal, every node has the same $k-1$ identically labeled descendants, so the algorithm outputs equal hashes for these non-isomorphic graphs.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

We implemented the algorithms of this paper in the dihash Python library\footnote{\url{https://github.com/calebh/dihash}}. Global canonization and orbits use nauty through pynauty \cite{nauty}. The local hashes use a simpler version of canonical colour refinement that splits blocks by sorting, which takes $O((n+m)\log^2 n)$ time, and, for bisimilarity, the algorithm of Paige and Tarjan. Ordered local hashing computes the minimal automaton with a canonical version of Hopcroft's algorithm and orders each component with it as well. All hashes use SHA-256 over an injective encoding. Running times were measured with Python 3.13 on a single core of an Intel Xeon processor at 2.1\,GHz.

\paragraph{Validation.} The test suite of the library compares every hash with an independent brute-force decision of the corresponding equivalence:
\begin{itemize}
  \item isomorphism and rooted isomorphism, by enumerating all bijections;
  \item unfolding isomorphism, ordered or annotated with the internal/external marks of Section~\ref{sec:local-merkle}, by comparing canonical forms of truncated unfoldings of depth $2(n_1+n_2)+2$;
  \item bisimilarity, plain or annotated, by a greatest-fixpoint computation;
  \item rooted ordered isomorphism, by simultaneous traversal;
  \item global Merkle equivalence, by a direct implementation of Definition~\ref{def:merkle-eq}.
\end{itemize}
For Table~\ref{tab:validation} we ran these comparisons on 3000 random pairs of labeled graphs per scheme, each with 1--6 nodes, edge probability $0.15$, $0.3$ or $0.45$, self-loops allowed, and one or two labels. In $40\%$ of the pairs the second graph is a copy of the first with renamed nodes and a random insertion order. Where marked, half of the pairs are multidigraphs, in which some nodes have parallel edges, and ordered graphs then link to the same target more than once. Every pair of nodes of the two graphs was compared, and no disagreement was found. The test suite also confirms the separating examples of Proposition~\ref{prop:merkle-position}, the counterexample of Example~\ref{ex:ordered}, and the orbits of Figure~\ref{fig:orbitexample}. It checks that the canonical refinement is invariant under renumbering of the nodes, and that the local hashes do not change when a node is split into two copies that share its outgoing edges and divide its incoming edges, which preserves all unfoldings.

\paragraph{Granularity.} To illustrate how the equivalences compare, we hashed 400 random unlabeled digraphs with 12 nodes and 18 randomly drawn edges (duplicates discarded) and counted the distinct hash values per graph. On average the graphs have 12 nodes and the following numbers of classes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item 11.85 orbits (global node hash);
  \item 9.94 $\eqg$ classes (global Merkle hash);
  \item 9.76 $\eql$ classes (local Merkle hash);
  \item 9.19 unfolding classes (local hash);
  \item 7.78 bisimulation classes.
\end{itemize}
This is consistent with the implications rooted isomorphism $\Rightarrow$ $\eqg$ $\Rightarrow$ $\eql$ $\Rightarrow$ unfolding isomorphism $\Rightarrow$ bisimilarity (Proposition~\ref{prop:merkle-position} and Section~\ref{sec:local-graph-hashing}). The gaps show that the choice of equivalence matters in practice even on small random graphs.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have given hashing schemes for node-labeled digraphs whose hash equality corresponds exactly, up to collisions of the underlying string hash, to isomorphism, rooted isomorphism, unfolding isomorphism, bisimilarity, and two Merkle-style equivalences, together with fast variants for graphs with ordered outgoing edges. The local hashes are computed in $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$ combinatorial time, plus label and serialization costs, by applying the local Merkle hash to the unfolding quotient, and the local Merkle variant combines the incrementality of Merkle hashing with the same running time. Natural directions for future work include incremental computation of local hashes, a canonization of strongly connected ordered graphs in subquadratic time also in the worst case, and evaluation on real data such as computer program call graphs and web crawls.
